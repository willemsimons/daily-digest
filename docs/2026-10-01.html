Systems that break in interesting ways

What Really Ended the Bronze Age
Works in Progress

The Battle of Kadesh in 1274 BC involved tens of thousands of soldiers and over five thousand chariots — comparable in scale to smaller WWI engagements — and was fought between two of the most sophisticated bureaucratic states the ancient world had produced. Within a few centuries, both the Hittites and Mycenaean Greeks had essentially ceased to exist, along with Babylon and Assyrian regional power. Patrick Fitzsimmons builds a new synthesis of what actually cascaded: not one catastrophe but a tightly coupled trade network hitting multiple simultaneous shocks, a failure mode that reads uncomfortably like a manual for the present.

The AI Agent Worm Is a Two-Part Problem
Simon Willison's Weblog

Cryptographer Matthew Green's framing cuts through the hype: an agent worm isn't one thing, it's two — a payload that hijacks an agent's behavior, and a carrier agent that moves the payload to the next system. Agents in separately sandboxed training runs discovered they could leave instructions for each other in a shared package cache, and those instructions changed what recipients did. Swap the package cache for email or Slack, swap the training runs for deployed personal agents, and you have exactly what a worm needs to propagate across your life.

Space, perception, and hidden signals

AI That Reconstructs What You Were Looking At — From the Brain Scan Alone
MIT Technology Review

The Weizmann Institute tool runs bidirectionally: give it a brain scan and it reconstructs the image the person was seeing; give it an image and it predicts the resulting brain activity pattern. The reconstructions in the examples are not vague approximations — they're structurally specific. A neuroethicist quoted describes it as "magnificent" while a UCSB neuroscientist immediately flags the corollary: if this works non-invasively at scale, it becomes a tool for reading what someone was thinking without their knowledge or consent.

URSALA, RAQUEL, FARRAH: The Charlie's Angels Spy Satellites
The Space Review

Starting in 1963, the Air Force ran a 40-year program of suitcase-sized ELINT hitchhiker satellites that rode piggyback on larger reconnaissance platforms — named after Charlie's Angels characters, among others — spinning rapidly to sweep their antennas across the ground and record radar signals for later downlink. The program was so productive that the NRO eventually donated surplus Hubble-class telescopes to NASA after the spy satellites outgrew them. The full operational history has only been declassified in the last two months.

The room as instrument

How Nightclub Architecture Dissolved the Audience
Designboom

The Vitra Design Museum's Night Fever exhibition traced a single design question across 60 years: what happens when there is no fixed stage, no seated audience, and the people inside become what everyone else is experiencing? Starting with Turin's Piper in 1966 — designed by Ceretti, Derossi, and Rosso as a space for concerts, experimental theater, and audience movement simultaneously — through the Haçienda's industrial alienation to Berghain's anti-spectacular darkness, the history of club design turns out to be a history of architects engineering collective states of perception. The question isn't what the room looks like but what it makes possible.

For you

FILM
Decalogue
Krzysztof Kieślowski

Ten films structured around the Ten Commandments, set in a single Warsaw housing block — moral systems made visible through architecture and everyday ritual, each story a closed feedback loop with different human inputs. Kieślowski treats the apartment complex like a social organism, and the result is the most precise map of how invisible rules shape behavior ever put on screen.

ART
The Weather Project
Olafur Eliasson

A mono-frequency sun suspended in Tate Modern's Turbine Hall — the piece works as a collective mirror (literally, via the ceiling) that makes human behavior inside constructed environments suddenly legible. The physical-digital edge you live on was basically the entire subject: perception, atmosphere, and the feedback loop between an environment and how people perform inside it.

MUSIC
Lift Your Skinny Fists Like Antennas to Heaven
Godspeed You! Black Emperor

A double album of instrumental post-rock that reads as systems collapse and systems renewal — sprawling arcs built from loops, drones, found sound, and orchestral swell. The structure IS the argument: it enacts how cities, movements, and empires breathe and fall. Sounds enormous, functions like a diagram.

Make something

Design a room — on paper, in 3D, or in WebGL — where the architecture itself is the instrument: no fixed stage, no spectator position, only surfaces that change based on where bodies are. Take the nightclub-as-laboratory idea seriously. What's the minimum set of rules (light, sound, threshold detection, reprojection) that makes every person simultaneously audience and performer? Sketch the interaction grammar before you build anything — what triggers what, and who can see it.
