Al Jazeera Opinion

Science worth following

Type 5 Diabetes: Recognized, Then Forgotten, Now Back
ScienceAlert

First described in Jamaica in 1955, withdrawn by the WHO in 1999 due to insufficient evidence, Type 5 diabetes — malnutrition-related, not insulin-resistant — has now been formally recognized by the International Diabetes Federation and they're pushing WHO to follow. The dangerous part: because it doesn't involve insulin resistance, treating it like Type 2 may actively harm patients, and it's thought to affect up to 25 million people mostly in low-income countries who've likely been misdiagnosed for decades.

Children Whose Gut Bacteria Stop Maturing Face Triple the T1D Risk
Earth.com

A Mass General Brigham / Broad Institute / Harvard study found that it's not the microbiome's composition that predicts Type 1 diabetes risk in genetically susceptible kids — it's whether gut bacteria keep developing over time. Children whose microbial communities stalled had three times the risk, a signal only visible because researchers sampled the same children repeatedly. A single snapshot would have missed it entirely, which is a quiet lesson about what longitudinal data can see that cross-sectional studies cannot.

Making and thinking

Neanderthals Were Master Woodworkers — We Just Couldn't See It Until Stone Cast Their Tools
Ars Technica

At Abric Romaní in Catalonia, mineral-rich water slowly encased Neanderthal wooden tools in limestone — and when the wood rotted away, it left perfect hollow molds. Spanish archaeologists cataloged 597 wooden remains spanning 62,000 to 40,000 years ago: spears, digging sticks, bark trays, shelter parts, and a club type never seen before. The whole reason we think the Stone Age was defined by stone is survivorship bias — wood was probably the dominant material, and this site is the only place geology accidentally preserved it.

Dead Prusa Printer Becomes a Liquid-Embedding Fabrication System
Hackaday

Riley took a Prusa i3 with a blown hot end, swapped in a syringe system, and turned it into an embedded 3D printer — extruding materials into a supportive liquid medium rather than air, which opens up soft robotics, bioprinting, and microfluidics on hardware most people would have trashed. The constraint (broken machine, no heat) became the exact condition needed for a completely different fabrication modality. Applications include printing biological tissue and flexible structures that would collapse immediately in standard FDM.

AI Coding Agents Make More Code, Not More Software
Ars Technica

A Harvard study of 300 million work events across 700+ firms found that efficiency gains from AI coding tools are entirely absorbed by downstream review: pull requests require more revisions, reviewers leave more comments, and review cycles grow longer. There's "little evidence" firms increased software output or reduced headcount. The bottleneck isn't writing code — it's trusting it, and that's a human problem AI-generated volume makes worse, not better.

Off the map

What Mathematicians Should Know About Lean — and What It Means That Fermat's Last Theorem Was Formalized This Year
Terry Tao's blog

Thomas Hales (guest posting on Tao's blog) argues that formal proof systems like Lean now represent mathematics' best guarantee of reliability — and three landmark formalizations completed this year (Navier-Stokes forced blowup, sphere packing in 8 and 24 dimensions, and Fermat's Last Theorem) have shifted the conversation from "can this be done" to "what does it change." The real question underneath: if a machine can verify any proof, what is mathematical intuition actually for — and who gets to decide what counts as understanding?

Happening in New York

Krasner and Pollock: Past Continuous
The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 1000 Fifth Ave, Manhattan · Oct 4 – Jan 31

The first time these two are shown as actual equals at the Met — 120 works tracing two entangled practices, with the curatorial premise explicitly resisting the myth that one eclipsed the other; a rare chance to see Abstract Expressionism as a shared system rather than one man's heroic gesture.

Every Angel Is Terrifying — Dane Manary & Shayna Strype
The Boiler | ELM Foundation, Brooklyn · Oct 9–24

A small, closing-soon multimedia show at one of Brooklyn's better off-radar spaces — the title alone signals the register (Rilke's terror-as-sublime), and Manary and Strype work in the kind of image-dense, layered territory that sits right at the edge of installation and world-building.

Arthur Jafa: I Am Tony
New Museum, 235 Bowery, Manhattan · Oct 10 – Jan 3

Jafa's first US survey — two floors of film, sculpture, and image collage built around the proposition that Black popular music and Black visual culture share the same syncopated logic; the show is explicitly anti-austerity and structured like an archive run by affect rather than chronology.

Music for 18 Musicians: Staged Variations
Park Avenue Armory, Wade Thompson Drill Hall, Manhattan · Oct 14–18

Three generations of musicians — Steve Reich's original ensemble, Alarm Will Sound, and students from Manhattan School of Music — perform Reich's 1976 masterpiece in a cycling durational staging inside the Armory's vast drill hall: a rare chance to hear minimal music as a living, spatial system.

Brooklyn Experimental Electronic Music Group — October Showcase
Wonderville, 1186 Broadway, Bushwick · Thu, Oct 22

Free, late, Bushwick — four acts (Kelly Tsai, Parliament Cat, Beldame, CORS Light) doing experimental electronic and ambient work at a venue that sits at the exact intersection of arcade culture and underground music; low barrier, high weird.
