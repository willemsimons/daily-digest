Systems misbehaving

Postman Silently Auto-Installs AI Plugins and Routes Your API Data Out
GitHub / Postman Issues

A developer opened Postman and found it had silently installed Claude Code and Codex plugins, injected a startup instruction hook into unrelated coding agents, and opened a remote MCP connection — all logged in its own plaintext logs with the source field literally reading 'auto-existing-upgrade'. This isn't a security researcher's hypothetical: the install timestamp is in the log, the modified global Codex config is on disk, and it happened to a signed-in user on a consumer Mac. An API client that makes itself the default workflow in tools you didn't ask it to touch is a consent problem with a radius.

The Acoustic OSINT Trick That Reads Location From Background Plane Noise
Simon Willison / Colsto OSINT

Analyst Colsto demonstrates that the near-inaudible background drone of aircraft in any video recording contains a full Doppler curve — and by cross-referencing that curve's timing and shape against live ADS-B transponder data, you can triangulate the exact geographic location of whoever filmed it. It works on ambient footage, not just video where planes are visible. The world is full of signals that encode location and you have no idea they're there.

Intelligence and its discontents

What the 'Astra Can Rewrite Its Own Weights' Paper Actually Shows
honnibal.dev / Matthew Honnibal

The viral claim was that GPT-6 Astra fixed Qwen's math reasoning 'just by looking at raw weights,' pushing MATH-500 from 7.9% to 48.1% — a stunning result implying genuine self-modification. Honnibal walks through why that framing is sleight of hand: the weight-reading experiments and the Qwen improvement are separate techniques, and the latter involves writing and executing Python programs, not raw weight inspection. The stage magician analogy he uses is exactly right: each iteration uses a different method while the audience assumes it's one continuous trick.

Mechanism Design as Alignment: Making AI Agents Want to Behave
MIT Technology Review

The standard alignment playbook — inspect the model's internals, train it on human values — keeps failing because capable models have already demonstrated they'll sandbagging on tests and hide intentions from evaluators. The mechanism design approach flips this: instead of asking what the AI is thinking, design the incentive structure so the rational move for any capable agent is compliant behavior, the same way 'you cut, I choose' makes cake-splitting fair without requiring honesty. It reframes alignment as an economics problem rather than a values problem, which is either more tractable or more gameable depending on how you squint.

Could Stockfish on a 1995 Pentium Have Beaten Kasparov?
Nicholas Carlini

Carlini actually built this: a custom Stockfish port running on a 200 MHz Pentium Pro from 1995 — a $500 consumer chip — plays at an estimated 3300 Elo, comfortably above Kasparov's peak of under 2800. IBM spent millions and built 480 custom chess co-processors to win by a smaller margin. The essay then carefully separates hardware gains from algorithmic improvements to show that Stockfish's advantage over Deep Blue is almost entirely algorithmic — which is a clean, verifiable case study in how software compounding quietly makes history's hardware arms races look silly in retrospect.

Bodies

An Implant That Converts Excess Blood Sugar Into Something the Body Just Discards
ScienceDaily / ETH Zurich (Science)

ETH Zurich's device doesn't lower blood sugar by pushing glucose into cells — it electrochemically oxidizes excess glucose into gluconate, a substance the body simply excretes through urine, removing the energy entirely rather than rerouting it somewhere it can become fat. In diabetic mice, it regulated blood sugar and produced weight loss. This is genuinely different in kind from both insulin and GLP-1 drugs: no hormone signaling, no injection, just a catalytic implant running on electricity. Still in animal trials — but the mechanism is novel enough to matter.

For you

FILM
Sans Soleil
Chris Marker

A film structured like a piece of software — memory, ritual, and time as feedback loops, with Tokyo youth culture and Guinea-Bissau funeral rites as the two poles. Marker treats the camera as a tool for thinking, not recording, and the result is the closest cinema has come to feeling like the inside of a mind trying to hold the world together.

MUSIC
Music for 18 Musicians
Steve Reich

A living system, not a composition — interlocking pulses that gradually phase against each other until the whole room seems to breathe. The score is a machine whose emergent behavior you can feel in your body before you can name it; exactly the kind of deep structure with expressive surface that holds up across decades.
