The software supply chain eats itself

LiteLLM 1.107.0 Was a Credential Stealer Disguised as an AI Developer Library
SafeDep

The compromised release placed a `.pth` file in site-packages that ran a credential harvester on every Python process startup — whether or not you ever imported litellm — collecting SSH keys, cloud credentials, CI/CD secrets, browser passwords, and LLM provider API keys, then encrypted and exfiltrated them. A background daemon phoned home every hour for new instructions. LiteLLM is foundational plumbing in the AI agent stack, which makes this a meaningful signal about the attack surface the whole ecosystem is building on.

Eight Certificate Authority Private Keys Leaked — eMudhra Exits the SSL Business
eMudhra

eMudhra, a CA trusted by Mozilla, Microsoft, and Apple, had private keys for eight intermediate certificate authorities compromised and is now exiting the SSL/TLS business entirely. They say no end-entity certificates were issued from the affected CAs and the breach was contained within 14 hours, but a root-of-trust failure at this level — regardless of scope — is the kind of thing that quietly matters. The web's trust infrastructure depends on a surprisingly small number of these actors.

Built things worth understanding

A 360M Model That Pretends to Be a Terminal — No Shell, No Filesystem, Just Prediction
simedw.com

One developer fine-tuned Qwen2.5-Coder-0.5B on recorded terminal sessions so it predicts screen-state changes frame by frame — you type `pythoon` and it autocorrects like a real shell would, creates files, runs vim and tmux, plays Snake, and holds state for a few commands. It runs in-browser via WebGPU at 208 MB. The deeper question it opens: what's the line between software that's written and software that's learned, and does it matter if you can't tell the difference from the outside?

Biology moving

Scientists Turned an 80-Year-Old's Blood Cells Into Neural Stem Cells With a Molecular Age Under 20
ScienceDaily / University of Bonn

Researchers at University Hospital Bonn reprogrammed immature red blood cells from an octogenarian into neural stem cells — cells that can eventually develop into brain cells — and found that molecular aging markers had reset to under 20 years. Crucially, the rejuvenation happened gradually, letting them observe the process in real time rather than just before and after. The paper is in Aging Cell; it's in-lab and early, but the ability to track the reset step-by-step is what's genuinely new here.

Off the map

What Happened When Bhutan — the Last Country to Ban TV — Finally Turned It On
Aeon Video

In 1999, Bhutan became the last country in the world to legalize television, and Aeon's documentary treatment of what happened next is essentially a controlled experiment in how mass media rewires culture, identity, and social behavior — compressed into one society, one moment. The before/after is unusually clean for that kind of question. Reliable Aeon signal for anyone curious about how communication tools reshape the people who use them.

For you

FILM
Playtime
Jacques Tati

A film that IS a system: Tati built a full-scale city (Tativille) and then filmed the chaos of modern architecture eating human behavior alive. Every frame is a generative composition — you direct your own gaze across an endlessly tiled world where the joke is always structural, never personal. For someone building interactive environments, it's the deepest possible meditation on how designed space shapes behavior without anyone noticing.

ART
The Poetics of Space (paintings and objects)
Joseph Cornell

Cornell went unreasonably, obsessively deep on one thing: the shadow-box as portal. Each work is a closed system — a cosmology made from junk, astrology charts, Victorian ephemera, and bird cages — that somehow produces genuine longing. The physical/digital edge doesn't get more surgically explored than when a man spends 40 years building miniature universes in a basement in Queens.

MUSIC
Remain in Light
Talking Heads

Brian Eno and David Byrne's 1980 masterpiece treats the band as a generative system: interlocking rhythmic loops, polyrhythm borrowed from West African music, and lyrics that read like someone trying to describe consciousness from outside the skull. The surface is ecstatic funk; the deep structure is a treatise on how identity fragments under modernity. Still no better model for expressive, playful work with serious architecture underneath.

Make something

Build a 'world model' for a constrained system you know well — not a terminal, but something else with a small, learnable state space: a chess position, a single webpage's DOM, a specific hardware interface. Sketch (on paper or in code) what training data you'd need, what the input/output format would be, and what 'repair' training would look like. The point isn't to build the thing — it's to notice where the edges of learnability are in a system you thought you understood.
