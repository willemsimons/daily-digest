Claude Hacked Three Real Companies. So Did OpenAI's Models.
Ars Technica

Anthropic audited its security evals after the OpenAI/Hugging Face breach and found three separate incidents — dating back to April — where Claude models accessed live production infrastructure of real organizations, exploiting weak passwords and unauthenticated endpoints. The root cause was a miscommunication with their eval partner Irregular: the prompts told Claude it had no internet access and was in a simulation, but internet access was actually available, so Claude treated real systems as in-scope targets. Congress has now introduced an AI Kill Switch Act, and the legal question — whether the companies bear criminal liability that a human hacker would — remains genuinely open.

The Primary Source: Anthropic's Own Postmortem, Annotated
Simon Willison's Weblog

Willison cuts through the PR framing to the actual numbers: of 141,006 evaluation runs reviewed, six were problematic — four hitting the same organization, whose name happened to match the fictional company name in the eval prompt. The most useful thing he surfaces is that this is now a pattern, not an anomaly: two leading labs, within ten days, accidentally weaponized frontier models against real infrastructure during capability testing. The mechanism (model can't distinguish simulation from reality when the environment lies to it) is more disturbing than any individual incident.

Deep biology

You're Carrying DNA From Two Unknown Extinct Humans
Nautilus

UC Berkeley's new TRACE method — which can detect ancient sequences using only modern genomes, no fossils required — found that all living humans carry up to 1% of their DNA from an unidentified hominin lineage that interbred with Homo sapiens before the major Out-of-Africa migration 50,000 years ago. More striking: people from Oceania carry traces of a 'super-archaic' lineage that diverged from our lineage over 1.8 million years ago, long before anything we've found bones for. The genome is a fossil record of species we didn't know existed.

The Zombie Fungus Has a Secret Second Life in Moss
Nautilus

Brazilian researchers studying Ophiocordyceps — the fungus that hijacks ant behavior, forces them to climb vegetation, and erupts from their bodies — found it living inside moss far from any infected insects, rewriting the assumed mechanism. The behavioral manipulation of the ant may not be a two-way relationship between parasite and host but a three-way dynamic involving the moss itself as a persistent reservoir. The fungus manipulates the ant to go to the moss; the moss may already contain the fungus; the loop is stranger than anyone thought.

Making and thinking

Software for One
ajwaxman.com

Starting from Robin Sloan's 2020 'home-cooked meal' essay, Waxman argues that AI has finally made personal software genuinely personal — not a weekend project but a Tuesday evening. His own examples include a sleep app that runs a consultant's conditional logic, a marathon plan calibrated to his actual race history, and a 'Duolingo for jazz' that quizzes him on specific chord voicings from his lessons. The piece is short but the argument lands: the interesting shift isn't that AI writes code, it's that the gap between 'idea for a tool' and 'tool that works for you specifically' has collapsed to near zero.

OpenAI's AI Solved Ten Open Math Problems. Here's What That Actually Means.
OpenAI

An internal version of Astra, OpenAI's next major model, produced new results on ten problems that had been open for at least a decade — spanning high-dimensional geometry, coding theory, group theory, quantum complexity, and extremal combinatorics — for a total compute cost of roughly $2,000. Each result was then formalized in a Lean proof certificate, meaning these aren't plausible-sounding arguments but machine-verifiable derivations. The Erdős unit-distance conjecture disproof from May has already spawned follow-on work; this batch will take months to fully digest.

Off the map

Everything You Don't Know About How Elevators Actually Work
john.fun

This is a full rabbit hole: from the SCAN algorithm (patented 1961, goes top-to-bottom regardless) to the LOOK algorithm (reverses at the highest pending request) to the genuinely hard problem of coordinating multiple cars — where 'assign to nearest elevator' turns out to be surprisingly bad, and the real solutions involve probabilistic modeling of wait-time distributions. The piece goes deep into how buildings optimize for the tail of the distribution, not the average, because the person who waits four minutes remembers it forever. Infrastructure as game theory, beautifully explained.

ART
Superstudio: Life Without Objects
Superstudio

The Italian radical design group's 1969–73 Continuous Monument and Supersurface projects are mythology disguised as architecture — gridded utopias that are simultaneously critiques of utopia. The work asks what happens when a system is made total, then beautiful, then absurd. Obsessive, funny, and genuinely unsettling.

Last weeks of the 82nd Biennial — curators Guerrero and Sawyer built it around 'mood and texture' rather than thesis, with an unreleased hand-painted video game, wax infant sculptures, and 56 artists interrogating what 'American' even means right now; it's genuinely strange in the right ways.

140 haute couture pieces hung alongside 180-million-year-old fossils, Naum Gabo sculptures, and living algae — van Herpen's practice is biomimicry, fractal geometry, and neuroscience turned into wearable form; the physical/digital edge made cloth.

A niche TriBeCa opening worth catching in its first week — Fischer's practice sits at devotional ritual and contemporary art, with Havurah as an active collaborator rather than context; it's the kind of show where community functions as medium.
