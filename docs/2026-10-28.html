Israel's Election: No Majority, Highest Turnout Since 1999
Atalayar / Reuters

Israel's first election since October 7 ended with 77.7% turnout — the highest since 1999 — and no one close to a governing majority: Netanyahu's bloc sits around 54 seats, Eisenkot's at 49, with Palestinian parties winning 17 seats in their strongest performance ever. Both leading candidates have ruled out governing with those Palestinian parties, meaning neither can reach 61. The result lands at the worst possible moment for anyone trying to coordinate a coherent regional posture ahead of a possible Iran operation.

Making and Fabrication

The Grid: A Factory-Scale Robot That Builds PCBs On Demand in Massachusetts
CircuitHub

CircuitHub's Grid uses magnetically levitating movers to carry each board on its own unique route through solder paste printing, component placement, reflow, and inspection — no changeovers, no per-design setup, no minimum order. They've been running it quietly for five years and are now making it public; the pitch is that it handles high-mix, low-volume work (ten of this, a hundred of that, new revision next week) faster than Shenzhen at competitive cost. The physical-digital fabrication loop closing in hardware, at small-batch scale, in the US.

Tom Hegen's 'The Mineral Age': Aerial Photography of the Green Transition's Extraction Cost
Colossal

German photographer Tom Hegen spent six years flying over the sites that will supply the 6.5 billion tons of cobalt, copper, lithium, and nickel the world needs to mine before 2050 — and the resulting 247-image book makes the systemic violence legible as pure color and geometry: candy-pink lithium evaporation ponds in the Atacama, ochre nickel pits butting against Indonesian rainforest. Systems made visible from altitude is a particular kind of argument, and Hegen makes it without editorializing.

Delaware Proposes Letting AI Agents Run Entire Companies — With Owners Off the Hook
Ars Technica

Delaware's draft 'Artificial Intelligence Company' bill would create a new corporate entity class operated entirely by AI agents, with human owners shielded from liability for the AI's decisions — any lawful purpose except banking. House Oversight chair James Comer is now demanding documents and signaling a federal law to cap state AI regulatory authority, framing it as a liability-laundering vehicle. The structure being proposed here is genuinely novel: not AI as tool inside a company, but AI as the legally operative entity of the company.

Off the map

The 1607 Bristol Channel Flood and the Meaning-Making Machine of Early Print
The Public Domain Review

When a storm surge killed 2,000 people and inundated 518 square kilometers around the Bristol Channel in January 1607, London's presses immediately converted the disaster into divine judgment — pamphleteer William Jones had God's interpretation in print before the floodwaters receded. Erik Crnkovich's essay tracks how the Little Ice Age's chronic weather extremes and the booming early-modern news industry co-evolved to manufacture a genre: the providential disaster narrative, which framed every earthquake, flood, and apparition as a moral weathervane. It's a portrait of a society doing in broadsheets exactly what we do in social media — collapsing the incomprehensible into shared meaning at speed.

The Amarna Letters: Bronze Age Rulers' Petty Disputes and Pleas for Gold
Aeon Video

The Amarna Letters are 3,400-year-old clay tablets — diplomatic correspondence between Egyptian pharaohs and the rulers of Babylon, Assyria, and the Hittites — and they read like a group chat of thin-skinned leaders haggling over gold shipments and complaining about protocol slights. Aeon Video tends to be tightly produced and genuinely educational; this one earns its place on a day when you're watching two nuclear-adjacent powers eye each other across the Persian Gulf.

For you

FILM
Sans Soleil
Chris Marker

A first-person essay-film structured as letters from a wandering cameraman — it's less a documentary than a meditation on how memory, image, and ritual encode culture, traveling from Tokyo's vending-machine shrines to Guinea-Bissau's post-revolutionary ceremonies. Obsessive systems-thinking disguised as travelogue; the invisible forces you love are its entire subject.
