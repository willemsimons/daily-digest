Romanian Army Divers Blow Up Russian Drones Found Near Europe's Biggest Gas Project
Ars Technica

Two Gerbera-type drones of Russian design were found floating near Neptun Deep — the offshore Black Sea project that will make Romania the EU's largest gas producer — and Romania had to call Ukraine to confirm they weren't theirs before blowing them up. This is the third escalation tier in one month: airspace incursions, a residential drone strike injuring civilians in southeastern Romania, and now energy infrastructure probing. The Neptun Deep project is the kind of target whose disruption would hurt all of Europe, not just Romania.

Roadside Cameras Are Quietly Building Identity Graphs from Your Phone's Bluetooth
Ars Technica

SignalTrace, marketed by Leonardo, pairs with license plate readers to recognize recurring clusters of Bluetooth and device signals traveling together — so if you carpool enough times, your phone gets associated with a vehicle even without your name ever being queried. The system works in reverse too: a device signal seen at an unknown plate can be traced backward through previous co-occurrences to known vehicles and then to registered owners. This is infrastructure already marketed and tested; it doesn't require a warrant for the pattern-matching phase because it starts with anonymous signals.

How machines think (and how we see into them)

A Fields Medalist Sits Down with the AI That Just Solved Problems He Didn't Expect to See Solved
Timothy Gowers

Gowers writes this days after OpenAI announced solutions to ten major open problems — including the first construction of a non-sofic group (one of group theory's most important unsolved problems) and a proof that multicolor Ramsey numbers grow superexponentially, which Gowers didn't expect to see in his lifetime. His essay isn't a celebration; it's a careful attempt to map where LLMs still fall short and why speed advantage alone isn't yet producing a flood of results, which tells you something real about the shape of the remaining gap. This is the primary source, not a journalist summarizing it.

Stealing the Thoughts That LLMs Encrypt Before Sending You
Cryptography Engineering / stolen-thoughts.com

Matthew Green — a serious cryptographer — noticed that OpenAI's chain-of-thought blocks arrive signed and encrypted, which immediately raised the question: why would thinking blocks need signatures unless tampering had security implications? He spent a weekend probing it, published his findings, and a European research team then built a working attack from his post that partially decrypts those reasoning traces through timing and statistical methods. The excerpt from the actual recovered GPT-5.2 Codex reasoning is genuinely strange to read — internal monologue rendered visible, the model talking itself through a grep for AWS keys in a sanitize-git-repo task.

Compression Is Prediction — The Deep Equivalence That Explains Both ZIP and GPT
ngrok Engineering Blog

The post traces a clean line from run-length encoding through Shannon entropy to Kolmogorov complexity to show that a compressor and a language model are solving literally the same problem: predict the next symbol well enough to represent it in fewer bits. It's not a metaphor — the math is the same math. Good explanation of why LLMs can compress data better than traditional tools, and why that fact should change how you think about what 'understanding' might mean.

How to Scratch Holograms with a Pen Plotter and No Lasers
Jordan Matelsky's Blog

Starting with a deliberately oily phone smudge as an intuition pump for diffraction, Matelsky derives the geometry of hand-drawn holograms entirely from first principles before automating the scratch patterns on a pen plotter using CD cases as the medium. No lasers, no darkroom — just obsessive geometry and the insight that a hologram is fundamentally a record of interference patterns that can be computed and physically scribed. The piece earns its length because by the end you understand *why* it works, not just that it does.

Stuffed Animals Turned Inside Out Into Fleshy, Organ-Like Sculptures
Colossal

Seoul-born, Chicago-based Sungho Bae collects discarded stuffed animals from shops and disposal sites, deconstructs them, and rebuilds them over figurative armatures into sculptures that read as organs, muscle tissue, and amulets — the show is titled *Apotropaia*, after the ancient Greek concept of objects that ward off evil. The taxidermy eyes embedded in one piece called 'Dansu' (a cross-cut neck with feathers) sit at exactly the threshold between comfort object and body horror. The question the work keeps asking — why were these cherished things thrown away, and can their significance be reshaped — is genuinely open.

For you

FILM
Sans Soleil
Chris Marker

A film that thinks the way you work: images as systems, memory as architecture, the feedback loop between culture and the mind that records it. Marker moves through Tokyo, Guinea-Bissau, and Iceland treating the camera as an essay, not a document — the prototype for every interesting essay-film since. If you've seen it, see it again; if not, it's the one.

ART
Early work (Neon and Language pieces, 1966–72)
Bruce Nauman

Nauman turned the studio into a closed feedback loop and the body into a test instrument — his neon pieces are traps where language and light make each other unstable. The same obsession with systems, behavior, and the gap between a sign and its meaning that runs through your practice. The Walker's and MoMA's permanent collection pages have the deepest documentation.

MUSIC
Versions (2007)
Matthew Dear

A record that sits exactly at the seam where club music becomes something stranger — melodic, paranoid, architecture you can inhabit. Dear builds worlds with very few materials and then subverts the rules of those worlds mid-track. Good for making things.
