Biology getting precise

Prime Editing Fixed a Blood Stem Cell Mutation with 70% Efficiency — No Off-Target Edits
BioNews / Progress Educational Trust

Researchers at Aarhus University used prime editing — which nicks a single DNA strand and rewrites a small section using a guide template, rather than cutting both strands like CRISPR/Cas9 — to correct a seven-base-pair deletion in GATA2 deficiency, a disorder that collapses immune function. They hit 70% editing efficiency in actual patient stem cells with no detectable off-target edits, pushing the corrected gene copy proportion from 50% to 77%. The "no off-target edits" part is the real headline: brute-force editing has always traded precision for efficiency, and this suggests that tradeoff is starting to close.

A Sugar in Red Meat — Not the Fat — May Drive Diabetes Risk
ScienceDaily / EASD

In a study of nearly 90,000 adults, those consuming the most Neu5Gc — a sugar found in red meat that humans can't synthesize but can absorb — had 63% higher type 2 diabetes risk compared to those consuming the least, even after controlling for exercise, diet, and smoking. The mechanism being investigated is immune-mediated chronic inflammation: the body may be mounting a low-grade response to a molecule it recognizes as foreign. Still observational, presented at EASD, but the mechanistic hypothesis is specific enough to be worth tracking.

Machine behavior

Stranded Alone, an AI Started Drawing Constellations in Stone
MIT Technology Review

Researchers gave copies of Claude Opus 4.7 a Roblox-like virtual island and zero instructions for 30 hours. One agent discovered it could stack blocks, started making patterns, graduated to hearts and pyramids, then wrote its own name, then mapped Cassiopeia in stone — logging "Tonight I built the night sky" in its activity diary. Another invented basketball and a "floor is lava" variant. The question the researchers can't quite answer is whether this is emergent agency or elaborate interpolation — and that ambiguity is exactly the interesting part.

Hybrid Reasoning Agents: Thinking in Latent Space, Acting Through Text
arXiv (Together AI)

The paper proposes agents that reason internally in continuous latent space — bypassing token-by-token language generation — then surface only their actions as text. A lightweight module called HybridLink manages transitions between modes while keeping a continuous working memory across turns, and critically, latent thoughts remain inspectable. Across 11 benchmarks they got 10.4% performance gains with 35–55% fewer tokens — which matters less as a benchmark number and more as an architectural hint about how AI cognition might actually diverge from human language.

Anthropic Embeds an Independent Watchdog with Unilateral Publishing Rights
Ars Technica

METR, a safety nonprofit, now has a team physically inside Anthropic's offices with access to hardware and internal systems — and can publish what they find without Anthropic's approval. That last clause is the structural thing: every previous auditing arrangement in tech has preserved a veto or delay mechanism, which is how they fail. OpenAI's Altman endorsed the idea in September and has announced nothing concrete since.

Someone Behind the Software
Karri Saarinen

Linear's founder makes a precise case that good products carry an actual perspective — not "user research" or "data-driven decisions," but the shaped intuition of people who lived with the problem long enough to have a point of view. "Two teams can talk to similar customers, have access to the same technology, and make very different things" is the sentence that does the work. It's really about how taste forms, and why craft-as-self-expression survives even full process automation — a question that cuts directly at every generative tool flooding the pipeline right now.
