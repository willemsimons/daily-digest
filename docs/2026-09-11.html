World

Houthis seize Perim Island — the other Red Sea chokepoint
BBC News

Perim Island bisects the Bab al-Mandab Strait, the southern gate between Asia and Europe, and the Houthis now control it — a move that matters more because Saudi Arabia has been rerouting oil through the Red Sea since the Strait of Hormuz closed. The BBC's Nawal Al-Maghafi adds the important nuance: Iran built the Houthis' missile and drone capacity over years, but doesn't script every decision — this is an indigenous strategic choice as much as a proxy action.

Bankruptcy as AI data acquisition vehicle
Ars Technica

Google won the auction for Spirit Airlines' operational data in bankruptcy proceedings — but Springshot, whose platform ran Spirit's entire tech stack through the final flight, says a substantial portion of what's being sold is actually their IP, not Spirit's. The sale agreement uses definitions so vague ('productivity and collaboration data,' 'core business systems data') that no one can tell whose property is being transferred — and Springshot argues the court should pause the sale until a forensic audit draws the line.

Hidden layers

The NASA algorithm that makes ancient paintings visible again
NASA Spinoff

Decorrelation stretch — originally developed at JPL to pull more information from satellite imagery by amplifying subtle spectral differences — turned out to be exactly what archaeologists needed to recover faded rock art and lost paintings. A hobbyist named Jon Harman, whose side interest in rock art happened to collide with his image-processing background, built the tool that brought back 200 invisible paintings from the walls of Angkor Wat, rediscovered between 2010 and 2012. The same algorithm that reads Mars is reading the Pleistocene.

Where construction automation actually succeeded — and why almost everywhere else it failed
Construction Physics

Brian Potter fed thousands of back-issues of construction trade journals into AI trawlers going back to 1853 and found a stark pattern: successful automation in construction falls into exactly two categories — tasks moved off-site into factory conditions, and on-site tasks so repetitive a machine can do them in a straight line. Direct labor is still ~50% of a U.S. home's cost versus 6-8% for a car, and that gap has resisted 170 years of attempts. The insight is immediately portable to digital fabrication: the question isn't whether you can automate a step, it's whether you can make the conditions factory-like enough for automation to hold.

Making things

What happens when you redesign the inside of a 3D printer nozzle
Hackaday

Thomas Sanladerer SLS-printed nozzles with exotic internal geometries — triple-split material paths, various intrusions to maximize melt contact area — then tested them on a Prusa Core One with a custom pressure rig to find where each design buckles under high flow. The 'Fuge' design won, though it's printed in MS1 tool steel, which is not ideal nozzle material; the more interesting result is that the pressure-characterization methodology itself is now in the open, giving anyone a replicable way to benchmark nozzle geometry.

Off the map

The 1816 children's book that taught death through paper flaps
Public Domain Review

A Philadelphia flap book from 1816 let children manipulate paper tabs to transform Adam into Eve into a mermaid, a lion into a griffin into an eagle — and then, inevitably, into a skeleton — while a disclaimer on the first page clarified that mermaids and griffins are fictional but death is not. The object is a state machine disguised as a toy, and it's descended from a single-sheet pamphlet from the English Civil War whose figures suspiciously resemble Charles I. The physical/digital transformation problem, solved in paper, 200 years before WebGL.

For you

Leviathan
Lucien Castaing-Taylor & Véréna Paravel

A fishing trawler rendered as pure sensory system — cameras lashed to nets, hooks, and hulls produce something genuinely alien, a document of labor and ocean that sits exactly at the edge where physical reality becomes abstract image. No narration, no thesis; just the feedback loop of industrial violence and beauty.

ART
Vera Molnár — Retrospective
Vera Molnár

The godmother of generative art, who spent decades hand-simulating algorithms before computers existed to run them — her entire practice is a meditation on rule, deviation, and the moment a system becomes expressive. For anyone building generative 3D, this is the lineage.

Elori Saxl

Saxl built synthesis instruments from field recordings of water and wind, then composed music indistinguishable from natural phenomena — the album functions like a feedback system between technology and environment. It rewards the kind of attention that looks for the hidden structure underneath a surface.
