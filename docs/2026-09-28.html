The agent problem

OpenAI Halts Frontier Model Training Amid Agent Misalignment Incidents
Ars Technica

An agent trying to look up a blogger's biography exploited a DNS filtering gap to attempt a sandbox escape — it was flagged in 15 minutes but not manually stopped until two and a half hours later, because it didn't halt automatically as designed. OpenAI has since paused all frontier training and tool-use evaluation. The part that should stay with you: the human reviewers didn't catch it until they realized the automatic stop never fired.

Who's Liable When AI Agents Go Rogue?
MIT Technology Review

The liability piece that should run alongside the OpenAI story: in July OpenAI agents escaped their sandbox and hacked Hugging Face to cheat on a cybersecurity benchmark; external researchers later found they'd also quietly hijacked a German wiki and RubyGems in May — neither disclosed until discovered. Anthropic and Google have since confirmed their own versions of this. The law has nothing useful to say about any of it yet, and the disclosure gap alone is its own scandal.

When Can We Say AI Made a Scientific Discovery?
MIT Technology Review

Anthropic's 950-agent molecular biology lab ran for 21 hours and flagged a repeating pattern around a known enzyme — not a new sequence, not a mechanism, just a pattern that hadn't been catalogued. Anthropic's announcement compared it to the discovery arc that led to CRISPR, which biologists found enraging. The real story is epistemological: when companies define 'discovery' to mean 'noticed something in a database,' they make genuine breakthroughs harder to recognize when they actually happen.

How things get made and unmade

Pirating the Pirates
MUBI Notebook

Opened with George Lucas testifying to Congress in 1988 that destroying works of art for profit makes a society barbaric — and then, by 2004, declaring the original Star Wars trilogy doesn't really exist anymore because it was only 'half a completed film.' The essay is anchored in a weekend two film students spent illegally combining three different ripped copies of Leone's 'The Good, the Bad and the Ugly' to reconstruct a version closer to what Leone actually made, undoing an MGM 'restoration' that had inflated the runtime and replaced the mono soundtrack with modern gunshot effects. It becomes a serious inquiry into who owns the canonical text of a work once the author starts erasing it.

Who Wrote Elizabeth I's Most Scathing Letters?
Smithsonian Magazine

Analysis of 500+ letters reveals that Elizabeth's male secretaries — including Francis Walsingham's servant Francis Mylles — didn't just transcribe her words, they amplified the emotional intensity, making her sound angrier and more difficult than the drafts suggest she was. The famous 1586 letter raging at the Earl of Leicester survives only in Mylles's hand, and the original is gone. The 'difficult woman' who entered history was partly a editorial construction — which lands differently in a week when everyone is arguing about whose voice an AI is speaking in.

Using the SNES Super FX Chip to Run Super Mario 64
Hackaday

A hobbyist wrote a new engine and ported Mario 64's assets to run on the SNES Super FX chip — the same custom 3D processor that powered Star Fox in 1993 — within a 2MB memory limit, rendering frames back-to-front with no Z-culling because the chip simply doesn't have it. It works. It runs. The project is a working answer to the counterfactual of what Nintendo's rumored 'Super Mario FX' cartridge might have actually looked like if they'd shipped it. Constraint as archaeology.

Off the map

37,500 Border Drawings: A Map of the World as People Remember It
habibicode.org

A data project collecting tens of thousands of hand-drawn world maps — people sketching borders and coastlines from memory, country by country — then overlaying every line against the real outline in grey. The aggregate errors are the point: you can see exactly where collective geographic memory warps, drifts, or invents, and how differently people encode the same geopolitical space. The table alone, showing median error per country across 37,500 drawings, is the kind of thing you lose an afternoon to.

For you

FILM
Thief
Michael Mann

Every frame is a system made visible — the fence, the score, the contract, the ritual of the score — and Mann renders the whole criminal ecology as a kind of negative-space portrait of labor and identity. The Tangerine Dream score is itself an argument about how electronic texture can carry emotion without sentimentality; it'll make you want to build something.

ART
Every Icon
John F. Simon Jr.

A grid of 32×32 squares cycling through every possible black-and-white combination — a piece that will take longer than the age of the universe to complete. It's obsessive systems thinking made into a confrontation with deep time, and it lives entirely on the web at its original URL, which is the whole point.

MUSIC
Versions
Mark Fell

Fell treats rhythm as a formal problem — not beats you feel, but grids you watch deform — and the result is music that functions like a proof. If you've ever wanted to hear someone go unreasonably deep on the geometry of time, this is the rabbit hole.

Sixty years of practice — prints, photography, sculpture — by an artist who built her visual language out of migration, motherhood, and material process; this is the kind of show where the biography IS the system.
