World

Last U.S. troops leave Iraq
Democracy Now / multiple outlets

The Pentagon confirmed Wednesday that all U.S. forces have departed Iraq, leaving an air base in the north under a bilateral agreement — ending a presence that, with interruptions, ran from 2003 through today. The withdrawal lands while the U.S. and Israel are actively at war with Iran, and Iran-backed militias have been clashing with American forces inside Iraq for months, so 'leaving' is not exactly 'resolved.' A 70-year-old Baghdad resident told reporters it felt like being born again.

September 2026: the world today, as seen by one Polish guy
tomwojcik.com

A Polish software engineer writes a ground-level dispatch on what it looks like to live next to Europe's largest war while tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz has fallen by over 90 percent — the largest supply disruption the oil market has ever seen. His core argument is structural and worth sitting with: for thirty years the world swapped buffers for dependencies because a supplier is cheaper than a stockpile, and this year several of those dependencies were tested simultaneously. Not pundit prose — annotated reality from someone whose diesel price is downstream of a strait 4,000 km away.

Infrastructure, physical and otherwise

First U.S. construction permit for a small modular reactor — it's actually happening
GE Vernova / NRC

The NRC issued TVA a construction permit for a BWRX-300 SMR at Clinch River in Oak Ridge — the first such permit in U.S. history, breaking a 40-year regulatory standstill. This isn't a feasibility study or a letter of intent; it's the actual permit. The site is in Tennessee, the reactor design is GE Vernova Hitachi's boiling-water concept, and the permit came out of an unusually efficient review that the NRC is now holding up as a model for future advanced reactor applications.

How Delhi cut electricity loss from 50 to 5 percent
IEEE Spectrum

In the early 2000s, half the electricity entering Delhi's grid simply disappeared — stolen, lost in aging lines, unmetered. An IEEE Spectrum piece by a professor who lived through daily blackouts traces exactly how privatization, smart metering, and ruthless incentive redesign compressed that loss to 5% over roughly a decade. It's a story about making an invisible system legible and then fixing it — the mechanics of institutional reform on hard infrastructure, told from inside the problem.

Vermont's largest power source is hidden in 5,500 living rooms
BBC Future

Green Mountain Power leases two home batteries to Vermont residents for $55/month — cheaper than the $12,000 gas generator most were about to buy — and aggregates them into a virtual power plant that is now the state's largest single source of dispatchable power. One participant hadn't lost power once since 2024 despite Vermont's record storm years. The same decentralization logic that restructured media and compute is now running through the grid.

Tools, agents, and the GPU layer

AMD acquires World Labs (Fei-Fei Li's world model startup) for $8.2B
Ars Technica

AMD is buying World Labs — founded by Fei-Fei Li, Justin Johnson, Christoph Lassner, and Ben Mildenhall in 2024 — for $8.2 billion, folding spatial intelligence and 3D scene understanding directly into the chip stack. World Labs' Marble tool generates 3D spaces as Gaussian splats exportable to production pipelines; Atlas followed with different emphases. This is AMD's direct counter to Nvidia's Hugging Face acquisition and lands world-model capability at the hardware level — relevant if you work in generative 3D.

SDF vs. MSDF vs. Slug: GPU text rendering, now that the Slug patent is public domain
AlphaPixel Dev

Eric Lengyel's Slug algorithm — which renders glyph outlines directly in the fragment shader with no texture atlas and no per-frame tessellation — was patented in 2019 and released to the public domain on March 17, 2026. This piece uses that moment to do a proper tour of GPU text rendering from bitmap atlases through SDF through MSDF to Slug, including exactly where each technique wins and loses. If you've ever lived with the compromises of baked glyph textures in a WebGL context, this is the piece.

Off the map

Philip Sidney and the extreme (dis)graces of the parenthesis
The Public Domain Review

Erasmus called parentheses 'little moons'; George Puttenham praised them as 'figures of tolerable disorder' (if used not too often 'nor too thick'); Sidney considered them indispensable and used more of them than any other Tudor writer — then died mid-sentence, his unfinished epic hanging on an open parenthesis. Florence Hazrat's essay for the Public Domain Review is a rabbit hole about punctuation as contested expressive technology, and about how notation systems shape thought before anyone decides they're rules.

For you

FILM
Sans Soleil
Chris Marker

A film that operates as a system of memory — images from Tokyo, Guinea-Bissau, and Iceland knit into an essay on how civilizations encode time, ritual, and forgetting. Marker built a machine for thinking about images, and it still runs.

MUSIC
Music for 18 Musicians
Steve Reich

A piece that makes its own structure audible: interlocking pulses that phase and breathe until the listener feels the architecture shift underfoot. Pure systems thinking you can inhabit for an hour — and it reliably makes people want to build something.

ART
Merzbau
Kurt Schwitters

Schwitters spent years growing a sculptural environment through his Hanover apartment — obsessive accretion, secret grottos, relics of friends — until the house itself became the artwork. The original was destroyed; what survives are photographs and reconstructions, which somehow makes the idea more alive. The edge where private mythology meets physical construction.

Twelve monumental photographs of 9/11 sourced from the internet, then hyper-compressed, recolored, and enlarged to the scale of 19th-century history painting — Ruff built a system for transforming collective trauma into something that looks like abstraction, and it's brutal.

Salon 94, New York · Sep 16 – Oct 24

Hamilton — whose best work turns the body into architecture and vice versa — shows new pieces alongside a curated presentation of late Gaetano Pesce, the designer who was her deepest formal influence; two sensibilities in direct conversation.

Djordjadze builds installations from cast materials, found forms, and domestic residues — sculptures that feel like paused thoughts, physical and fugitive at once, with a surface casualness that hides serious structural thinking.

Dia Beacon, 3 Beekman Street, Beacon, NY · Oct 3 – Dec 31

576 white-lacquered rods arranged into 64 groups whose configurations are determined entirely by the hexagrams of the I Ching — De Maria used an ancient divination system as a sculptural logic, and the result fills two vast Dia galleries with something between minimalism and cosmology.
