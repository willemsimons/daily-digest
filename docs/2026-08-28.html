World

Federal judge rules Trump's blacklisting of Anthropic was illegal retaliation
Ars Technica

Judge Rita Lin found that after Anthropic refused to drop its restrictions on lethal autonomous weapons and mass surveillance of Americans, the Pentagon banned the company from all federal contracts — including ones unrelated to the military — and declared it a national security risk. Lin vacated the directives entirely, writing that 'the empty invocation of national security is not a blank check to punish.' The ruling is the clearest judicial line yet against using procurement power as speech suppression.

Inventati.org

Glacial barrier lake on the China-Nepal border is at critical stage
Kathmandu Post

An ice-rock avalanche on the Nepal-China border formed a landslide-dammed lake that is now overflowing, with the Chinese Embassy in Kathmandu warning downstream communities to monitor river levels and prepare for a possible breach. The embassy declined to say how large the lake is or how much water it holds — which is either diplomatic caution or a sign nobody knows. Glacial outburst floods in this terrain move fast and hit hard.

Infrastructure under pressure

AI chip demand has halted Pine64's Linux board production
Hackaday

RAM and eMMC prices have risen so much — demand pulled by AI infrastructure build-out — that Pine64 has suspended production of its Linux single-board computers rather than pass the cost on. This is a concrete, community-level casualty of the chip economy most coverage ignores: the open hardware ecosystem is being quietly starved by the same compute gold rush everyone is celebrating. The Hackaday piece notes microcontrollers (with onboard storage, thus insulated from the price spike) are now the more rational choice for many use cases.

Tracer.AI auto-DMCAed an open-source game engine off Google Play — on Microsoft's behalf
Luanti Blog

Luanti, a fully open-source voxel game-creation platform with no proprietary code or assets, was removed from Google Play after Tracer.AI filed a DMCA notice alleging Minecraft copyright infringement — apparently because it's blocky. This is the second time the same company has done this to Luanti (they won the 2023 appeal), and Tracer.AI filed a similar notice this year against an unrelated indie voxel game called Allumeria. The pattern is clear: AI-automated copyright detection is now generating legal force without human review, and platforms comply rather than scrutinize.

The rumor of a bug is now enough for AI agents to find the exploit
Anil Recoil

Anil Madhavapeddy released a security fix for the OCaml cohttp library and noticed exploit probes hitting his server with the exact bug pattern within minutes of opening the patch PR — before the advisory was even published. He then tested his own agents against the code and found DeepSeek V4 Pro independently found related issues and generated a working exploit in under a minute, armed only with a rough description of the problem area. The upshot: the coordinated disclosure window is effectively gone, and the open-source security response model was built for a world that no longer exists.

Things machines are doing now

Multi-agent AI systems are autonomously discovering new mathematics
arXiv

Researchers built 'the Station,' an open-world environment where AI agents from different model families pursue shared math research goals without a central coordinator — choosing their own directions, running experiments, and building a shared literature. Across 14 problems, the system produced results novel relative to prior literature on five of them, including a new infinite family of finite-field Kakeya sets and improved bounds on Erdős's minimum-overlap problem. Crucially, the agents produced not just numerical constructions but theorems explaining why the constructions work — which is what makes the results actually usable by human mathematicians.

GLM-5.3-Flash: 320B-parameter open model trained entirely on Chinese chips
The New Stack

Z.ai's GLM-5.3-Flash spent days as the most popular model on OpenRouter under the alias 'Ox-alpha' before being unmasked — competitive with Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.6 Terra at benchmark, priced at $0.075 per million input tokens, and MIT-licensed. The buried lede is that it was trained on domestic Chinese silicon, not Nvidia GPUs, which is the first credible evidence that the chip embargo is accelerating self-sufficiency rather than arresting it.

Off the map

DOOM played on a series of 555 timers — kind of
Hackaday

Nick built a circuit that plays the DOOM opening theme using nothing but 555 timers: one acts as a 7 Hz system clock, feeding a binary counter and decoder that steps through 15 positions, each mapped via a diode matrix to one of six pitches, which then drives another 555 tuned by resistor networks to produce sound. It's entirely discrete analog logic — no microcontroller, no firmware. The 'run DOOM on it' framing is technically a troll, but the actual achievement is a working hardware sequencer built from first principles, which is a different and realer thing.

Panoramic photography with a linear scanner — temporal distortion included
Hackaday

Philo repurposed a single-line industrial CCD (the kind used to inspect conveyor belts) as a slit camera, capturing one column of pixels at a time and stitching them with accelerometer-stabilized software as the camera travels along railways and boats. The output images are enormous and slightly wrong in a specific way: anything that moves relative to the camera's travel creates temporal smear artifacts that film panoramics can't produce. It's a tool inverted past its design intent and producing images that couldn't exist any other way.
