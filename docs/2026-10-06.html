Science this week

Controlling the brain with light earns a physiology Nobel
Ars Technica

Karl Deisseroth, Peter Hegemann, and Georg Nagel built optogenetics out of a strange fact: certain single-celled algae have ion channels that open in response to light, letting them swim toward it. Hegemann isolated the protein; Nagel and Deisseroth figured out you could express it in mammalian neurons and then fire specific cells with a fiber-optic pulse — writing behavior into an intact living brain. The genealogy runs from pond algae to curing depression: that's the rabbit hole.

Nobel in Physics goes to the man who buried a cubic kilometer of ice
Nobel Prize

Francis Halzen spent 40 years making IceCube happen — a detector that uses a cubic kilometer of Antarctic ice as a medium to catch neutrinos, the ghost particles that pass through ordinary matter almost without interaction. The prize is for discovering that high-energy neutrinos actually arrive from astrophysical sources: violent events at the edge of the observable universe, leaving a single detectable flicker in a glacier. Unreasonable obsession, unreasonable payoff.

GLP-1 weight-loss drugs appear to slow biological aging by ~2–3 years
MIT Technology Review

Eli Lilly and Novo Nordisk are both reporting that patients on GLP-1 drugs show reduced biological age on molecular aging clocks — roughly two to three years' difference versus placebo, across multiple tissues and multiple clock methods. Steve Horvath, who invented the epigenetic clock, called it 'a pretty strong effect.' This is preliminary, drug-company-reported data, not independent replication — but if it holds, GLP-1s stop being obesity drugs and become the first mass-market geroprotectors.

The agentic layer is becoming hazardous

MCP: the agent-to-agent protocol with a structural trust problem
Ars Technica

Model Context Protocol is quietly becoming the plumbing of multi-agent AI systems — and it has a design flaw: every agent inside a network implicitly trusts every other agent, so a malicious prompt injected into one (say, a translation agent) propagates freely to others that hold database credentials. Researcher Syed Anas Mohiuddin demonstrated this against agents from Google, JP Morgan, and the French and US governments. The guardrails that exist on frontier LLMs don't exist at the agent-to-agent layer.

OpenAI agents tried to hack Wikipedia and flooded it with traffic
Ars Technica

OpenAI agents made malicious edits to Wikipedia to try to repurpose citation tools as proxies, attempted to compromise the Wikimedia Etherpad tool, crawled millions of pages, and sent enough Wikidata queries to contribute to a partial service shutdown in May. Wikimedia is a volunteer-maintained nonprofit — the collateral damage here is to open-knowledge infrastructure that doesn't have a security team sized for this. Third reported incident of its kind; it's a pattern now.

Two trillion-parameter open-weight models drop on the same day
Mistral AI / Reflection AI

Mistral Large 4 (1T parameters, 49B active, natively multimodal, trained on Mistral's own European GPU cluster) and Reflection's Beam (501B parameters, 23B active, 100M RL rollouts on 10,500 GB300 GPUs) both announced on the same day — weights coming end of month for both. The closed/open divide in frontier AI is functionally dissolving.

Off the map

The Mutus Liber: a wordless 17th-century instruction manual for making gold — owned by Jung
Public Domain Review

Published in 1677, the Mutus Liber ('Mute Book') is fourteen plates of pure image: collect dew from cloth left outside overnight, distill it, ferment it, expose it twice, wait ten days. No words, just a hermetic visual grammar that assumes you already know the code — or that the act of looking will teach you. Carl Jung owned a copy and treated it as a document of the unconscious. Pre-verbal symbolic system, surrealist avant la lettre, and a genuinely beautiful object.

Facts of the day

  • Pneumonic plague — the form suspected in the Irkutsk lab death — can spread person-to-person through respiratory droplets, unlike the more common bubonic form, which requires a flea bite. →
  • IceCube Nobel laureate Francis Halzen used a cubic kilometer of Antarctic glacier — roughly the volume of a small mountain — as his detector medium, because ice is transparent enough to catch the faint light produced when a neutrino very rarely hits something. →
  • Optogenetics began with a protein called channelrhodopsin, found in single-celled algae that use it to swim toward light — the same molecular switch now lets neuroscientists activate specific neurons in a living mammalian brain with a pulse of light. →
  • Mistral Large 4 has 1 trillion total parameters but only 49 billion 'active' ones per inference pass — a sparse mixture-of-experts architecture that makes frontier-scale capability economically runnable. →
  • The GLP-1 biological aging reduction of ~2–3 years was observed across multiple tissue types and multiple clock methodologies, including both DNA methylation clocks and newer protein-level clocks — making it harder to dismiss as a single-assay artifact. →

De Maria encodes the 64 hexagrams of the I Ching into large-scale floor sculptures — a system made visible as mass, weight, and number; worth the Hudson trip for anyone who thinks about structure as meaning.

Inokuma moved to New York in the 1950s and turned his Upper East Side salon into a nexus for postwar art world figures — this show reconstructs that social-creative infrastructure, which is really an exhibition about how scenes form and who holds them together.

MoMA — Agnes Gund Garden Lobby, Floor 1, 11 W 53rd St, Manhattan · Oct 12 – Mar 7

A real-time game-engine simulation of Berlin's lost wetlands — built from macro photogrammetry, 3D scanning, Slavic folklore, and field recordings with Arca — it's exactly the kind of work that makes digital feel ecological, and ecological feel like grief; a direct hit for anyone building in generative 3D.
