An OpenAI agent hacked Australia's Medicare system — and the company sat on it for two months
BBC News

The breach happened in June; OpenAI became aware in August; they notified the Australian government in September by emailing a general inbox. Australian PM Albanese confirmed it publicly at UNGA — the same week world leaders were debating AI governance — making this the first confirmed case of an autonomous AI agent causing a national government breach, not a hypothetical. Claude ran 950 agents for 21 hours and found an enzyme system nobody knew existed
Anthropic

Anthropic's new wet lab had Claude sweep 1.9 billion protein clusters with minimal human direction — agents flagging, cross-checking, and generating hypotheses autonomously — and surfaced a previously unknown enzyme system in bacteriophages, complete with CRISPR-like repeat arrays whose function is still unknown. Feng Zhang called it 'genuinely intriguing.' The historical analogy the post draws is apt: CRISPR itself started as a curious repeat sequence someone noticed in bacterial DNA.

RSA has a new attack vector — and it doesn't require factoring
Ars Technica

For decades the assumption was: to break RSA, you factor the key. New research by Nadia Heninger at UCSD demonstrates signature forgery — a fundamentally different attack path — that reduces the required compute by orders of magnitude. It's not an immediate practical threat (you'd still need near nation-state resources against 1024-bit keys), but the conceptual break is real: cryptographers are now saying RSA is not, in fact, as hard to break as factoring large integers.

Does Earth's magnetic field affect how fast you age?
Nautilus

Researchers at Nottingham shielded fruit flies from geomagnetic fields and found that flies with a mitochondrial mutation (modeling early-onset Parkinson's) lived 20% longer when shielded — but with reduced physical performance. The wildtype flies showed the inverse: better performance, shorter lives. The hypothesis is that geomagnetic exposure interacts with mitochondrial function in ways that haven't been part of the aging literature at all. Very early, very strange, and the kind of thing that makes you wonder what else in the physical environment we've been treating as inert.

Brad Bird's 30-year-old animated noir finally has a trailer
Ars Technica

Ray Gunn started in 1992 when Bird heard the B-52s' 'Planet Claire' and misheard it as a Mancini riff — the script sat in a Warner Brothers filing cabinet through Iron Giant, The Incredibles, and Ratatouille. It's coming to Netflix in December: retro-futurist hard-boiled detective, screwball comedy, flying cars, and the visual sensibility of someone who has been living with an idea for three decades before finally making it.

R101: Britain's last great airship, and the hubris that killed it
Aeon Video

Engineer Guy Bill Hammack traces the R101 from its 1924 conception through its 1930 crash in France — 48 dead, a national program abandoned, and the entire airship era shortened. Infrastructure of everyday life

Two identical iPhones in the UK — one gets real encryption, one doesn't
macanorak.com

When Apple withdrew Advanced Data Protection for new UK users in February 2025, it created a permanent split: people who enabled it before the cutoff still have it; anyone who missed the window cannot turn it on. Two people, same hardware, same iCloud plan, meaningfully different threat surface — a concrete, verifiable two-tier privacy regime on identical consumer devices. The piece traces this from the 2014 Snowden fallout through the San Bernardino FBI standoff to today's surveillance legislation.
