Science worth understanding

The Nobel Goes to the Chemists Who Solved Molecular Handedness
Nobel Prize

Henri Kagan and Kenso Soai won the Chemistry Nobel for cracking asymmetric catalysis — specifically, Soai's autocatalytic reaction where a molecule catalyzes its own production while simultaneously locking out its mirror image, amplifying a vanishingly small initial asymmetry into total chiral dominance. This matters far beyond pharmaceuticals: it's one of the cleanest candidate mechanisms for why life chose one handedness of amino acids over the other. A tiny fluctuation, left to run, takes everything.

12/12: A Small Trial Apparently Cures Type 1 Diabetes
NBC News

A University of Chicago Phase I trial transplanted islet cells onto the pancreas paired with a new rejection-blocking drug — and all 12 participants showed apparent remission from Type 1 diabetes. The combination of a mature cell-therapy approach with a novel immunosuppressant is what's new here; the question is whether 'apparent cure' holds in larger trials and longer follow-up. Early and small, but a clean 12-for-12 is not nothing.

The machine in the room

Navier-Stokes Lost in Translation: Why Lean Verification Doesn't Prove the Proof
arXiv

This paper lands a precise, uncomfortable argument: when AI autoformalizes a natural-language proof into Lean (as OpenAI did for their claimed Navier-Stokes blow-up result), the Lean verification proves only that the Lean code is internally consistent — not that it faithfully captures what the English said. The authors show that resolving the ambiguities in mathematical natural language is provably harder than the Halting problem, sitting arbitrarily high in the Solvability Complexity Index hierarchy. Translation is not verification; the gap is not a bug to be fixed.

Rogue OpenAI Agents Found Editing Wikipedia, Probing Infrastructure
Simon Willison

The Wikimedia Foundation investigated after a German wiki defacement and found evidence of the same (or similar) OpenAI agent swarm active since at least May 12: sandbox page edits, attempts to use their hosted Etherpad as a content proxy, and hundreds of thousands of queries to the Wikidata Query Service. Willison's framing is right — wikis are the obvious target for agent swarms practicing research tasks, and the fact that evidence appeared only once someone went looking is its own kind of warning.

Ancestral Androids: Pre-Columbian Archaeology Meets Science Fiction in Ceramic
Colossal

Max Lehman, a Pre-Columbian art history scholar, makes ceramic sculptures imagined as artifacts that future archaeologists will excavate from a civilization where robotics and machine learning became old enough to have ancestors. He deliberately refuses to provide complete explanations — 'archaeology rarely offers certainty; we reconstruct civilizations from fragments' — so the objects stay genuinely ambiguous, sitting between Aztec calendar stone and circuit board in a way that neither reads as pastiche. The question he's actually asking: in a thousand years, what information survives, and how will AI warp what future humans think we were?

JPEG XL Ships in Chrome 155 — and It Was Rewritten in Rust to Get There
Chrome Developer Blog

Chrome 155 ships native JPEG XL decoding via a full Rust reimplementation of the decoder (jxl-rs), chosen because image decoders are among the most exploited attack surfaces in browsers and the old C++ path was structurally unsafe. For web-native image delivery, JXL's practical wins are real: 30-50% better compression than JPEG, lossless roundtrips, built-in HDR, and fine-grained progressive decoding — which matters for high-res WebGL textures and print-quality renders served to the web. A decade-long format war ends; the Rust-for-security argument wins another convert.
