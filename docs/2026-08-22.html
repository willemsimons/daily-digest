Canada-U.S. Trade Talks Collapse — 50% Tariffs Now in Force
Office of the Prime Minister of Canada

At midnight, the U.S. imposed 50% tariffs on $20 billion in Canadian goods after last-minute negotiations failed to meet Carney's threshold — tariff-free access preserved for most sectors, but not the key strategic ones. Carney's statement is notable for its unflinching acknowledgment that "America has changed" and the old relationship is gone, not paused. This is the biggest rupture in Canada-U.S. trade in living memory, framed by Ottawa not as a crisis but as a managed decoupling.

Felony Charges for Wiping Your Phone at the Border
New York Times

Samuel Tunick now faces felony obstruction charges for deleting data from his phone at a U.S. border checkpoint — establishing a potential legal precedent that digital self-erasure constitutes evidence tampering in the eyes of federal law. The case turns on whether you have a right to a blank slate at the border, or whether the act of wiping is itself suspicious. Whatever the outcome, it will define the legal perimeter of digital self-defense for years.

Infrastructure reading itself

I Accidentally Logged Hundreds of Thousands of Phone Calls to Military Bases
lina.sh

By registering unclaimed DNS zones in the e164.arpa system — an early-2000s scheme that converts phone numbers into domain lookups so VoIP can route calls over the internet — a researcher became an inadvertent passive wiretap for military base traffic. The system was essentially abandoned but never fully decommissioned, so its delegated zones expired and sat open. It's a perfect accidental-discovery story: obsolete infrastructure still trusted by production systems, a researcher who just wanted to poke at a curiosity, and a national-security implication nobody planned for.

Researchers Use Thunderquakes to Study the Earth's Crust
Ars Technica

Thunder's shockwave enters the ground as seismic energy — "thunderquakes" — and Penn State researchers built a model that finally extracts clear signal from it, reconstructing subsurface terrain under their campus using fiber optic cables already buried there. The complexity comes from lightning's structure: it fires as a string of plasma beads along its path, each acting as a separate seismic source. The same fiber networks that carry internet traffic can, it turns out, double as distributed seismometers — infrastructure reading the ground it runs through.

Mapping things

Scientists Release the Biggest 2D Map of the Universe
Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory

The DESI Legacy Imaging Surveys combined 263,000 telescope exposures into a 5.6-trillion-pixel map of 4 billion celestial objects — stars, galaxies, gravitational lenses — and the whole thing is publicly browsable through the Legacy Survey Sky Viewer right now. It's already the foundation for over 1,800 published science papers and the substrate for a 3D dark energy survey layered on top. Open the viewer and lose an hour.

Galactic Compass 2: Now with Augmented Reality
Matt Webb / Interconnected

Matt Webb's app — a floating arrow that always points toward the galactic center, 26,000 light-years away — now has an AR mode where you can place that arrow in physical space and walk around it. What's remarkable is the dispatch he includes from users: people in grief, sitting outside at night, finding something clarifying about having a fixed cosmological reference point when everything else is moving. The Earth spins; the Sun moves; but you can always know which way the black hole is.

The History of Cartography — Volume 6 Now Free Online
University of Wisconsin–Madison

The definitive scholarly series on maps as cultural artifacts — power, ideology, and technology simultaneously — has made Volume 6 (20th century cartography) freely available online, joining earlier volumes covering everything from prehistoric mapping to the European Enlightenment. The project's founding editor J.B. Harley essentially invented the idea of reading maps as ideological documents rather than neutral tools. If you've ever wanted the deep reading on how maps encode the worldview of whoever made them, this is the primary source.

Living matter, orbital matters

Brain Organoids Kept Alive for Five Years — And They're Still Developing
Nautilus

Most lab-grown mini-brains are discarded after their experimental purpose ends; an international team instead kept theirs running for five years, and found that after the first birthday the tissue hit developmental milestones that only normally occur postnatally — outside any body, in a dish. Cell types emerged in the right order, neural connection complexity increased, and gene expression followed expected timelines. At five years old, they're still going, and researchers had to invent new culture media to keep up with the tissue's increasing metabolic demands.

Space Mirrors That Shine as Bright as 10,000 Full Moons
MIT Technology Review

Reflect Orbital's test satellite launches later this year — an 18×18-meter mirror — as a precursor to a constellation of up to 50,000 larger (54×54-meter) mirrors that would beam sunlight on demand to Earth for solar charging, emergencies, and military use. A new Astrophysical Journal Letters paper calculates that the scattered light from these beams would be equivalent to 10,000 full moons and would bleed across tens of kilometers beyond the target zone, effectively ending dark skies for anyone nearby. The FCC approved it in July.

Off the map: Lou Benesch Paints on Found Antique Boards
Colossal

Paris-based Lou Benesch makes surreal mixed-media paintings on vintage board games and antique chalkboards — the aged surfaces themselves become part of the work's visual argument. The current show, closing today at Hashimoto Contemporary in San Francisco, takes the Tarot Magician as its frame: headless birds diving into a central star, a horse with a coiled serpent, a rabbit ringed by unblinking eyes. It's the kind of work where the choice of ground is as considered as anything painted on top of it.
