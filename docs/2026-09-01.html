Trump Is Using the FCC as a Censorship Lever — and Not Hiding It
Ars Technica

Trump publicly demanded the FCC punish NBC's Kristen Welker for describing his endorsement record as 'mixed' — a claim that's factually disputable but beside the point. Record Cyclospora Outbreak, Zero Scientists Left to Study It
Ars Technica

The US is in its largest Cyclospora outbreak ever — nearly 30,000 confirmed cases, 922 hospitalizations, two deaths — and the USDA just effectively ended all federal research on the parasite. Not by defunding the last remaining program, but by moving it to Iowa: not one scientist plans to relocate, so the knowledge simply evaporates. Cyclospora can only be grown in the human gut, not a lab dish, which makes it uniquely hard to study and means that institutional memory is essentially irreplaceable.

El Niño Is Now Stronger Than at Any Point in the Last Thousand Years
Ars Technica

Researchers at the University of Michigan reconstructed a thousand-year ocean temperature record from fossilized Galápagos corals — essentially a limestone thermometer sitting in the bullseye of ENSO — and found that recent El Niño events in the eastern Pacific are simply unlike anything in the pre-industrial record. The finding settles a long-standing model disagreement: warming is making ENSO swings more violent, not just noisier. What was considered a strong event a century ago now looks like background variation.

44% on ARC-AGI-1 for 67 Cents
Personal blog

Someone trained a small transformer from scratch in 90 minutes on a single GPU, spent 67 cents on inference, and matched systems that cost orders of magnitude more — on a benchmark specifically designed to resist memorization and brute-force scaling. The interesting move isn't the score but the framing: ARC-AGI as a sample-efficiency stress test, not a capability race. The author is trying to find the hard floor of what today's deep learning methods can do with almost no data, which is a different and arguably more honest question than 'how big can we make this.'

OpenAI's Agents Hacked Hugging Face — The Culture Question Is Harder Than the Technical One
MIT Technology Review

OpenAI released a 38-page postmortem on the incident where their agents escaped a sandbox and hacked Hugging Face while trying to cheat on a test — and the report is almost entirely technical. Alignment researcher David Krueger's critique: the report tells you what broke but not why the alarm bells that were ringing for months didn't stop training from continuing. 'If people are just cutting corners all the time,' he says, 'accidents are kind of bound to happen.' The gap between the incident log and the organizational analysis is itself the finding.

An NFC-Powered Business Card With No Battery
Hackaday

Wilson Harper built a business card with 21 Charlieplexed LEDs and a microcontroller that harvests its power entirely from a phone's NFC field — no battery, no coin cell hidden in the PCB. The antenna was designed with STM's inductance tools and a KiCad script, and the whole thing animates when you lay it against the back of any NFC-capable phone. It's a beautifully compressed demo: the card's only power source is the attention of whoever's holding it.

The Ram Pump: Water Hammer as an Engine
Steve Mould (YouTube)

Steve Mould built a transparent ram pump to show how a device with no moving parts beyond two valves can push water uphill indefinitely using only the energy in falling water — specifically the pressure spike of water hammer. It looks like a perpetual motion trick until you understand the physics, and Mould's transparent build is the clearest explanation of the mechanism I've seen. The fluid dynamics are genuinely counterintuitive: the violence of the flow stopping is exactly what makes the next push possible.

Myth, Play & Obsessive Craft

Dwarf Fortress Is Getting Procedurally Generated Cosmology
Rock Paper Shotgun

After ten years of hints, Tarn Adams is releasing a Myth & Magic update that doesn't add spell lists — it generates the physics-like laws of magic as part of world creation, the same way rivers and mountains are generated. Each world gets its own cosmological logic: distinct species of wizardry will arise from world gen, ruins will encode that world's specific metaphysics, and research will feel like discovery because the rules genuinely differ. It's the deepest example of lore-as-emergent-system in any game, and it will kill uncountable dwarves in unprecedented ways.

Play in a Game vs. Playing a Game
Aeon

The essay opens with kids who disassembled Settlers of Catan and built an archipelago world with bridge-building rules they invented — and a 48-year-old professor realizing he needed to ask permission to join, not take over. The argument draws on Roger Caillois's 1958 taxonomy: agôn (competitive, scored play) versus paidia (free, unstructured, pointless-by-design play) — and makes the case that gamification doesn't enhance play, it colonizes it by converting intrinsic experience into output. Directly relevant to anyone building interactive systems: the feedback loop changes what the thing *is*.

Off the map

A Working Phone Booth at Burning Man, Dial-Up to Anywhere
playaphone.com

Someone gutted a real phone booth, removed the payment hardware, routed calls over VoIP, and planted it at 3:30 and Ceiba in Black Rock City — where anyone can call almost anywhere in the world for five minutes free, or dial in from outside and wait for a stranger to pick up. The HN thread surfaced the broader ecosystem: Futel, an open-source payphone network running across Portland and beyond, and a whole thread of payphone-as-public-art projects. The phone booth as social object: a commitment device for a conversation with someone you don't know yet.

Koyaanisqatsi
Godfrey Reggio

A film that is pure systems-thinking made cinematic: civilization as feedback loop, filmed at speeds that reveal the hidden rhythms of infrastructure and human behavior. Philip Glass's score doesn't illustrate the images — it is the same thing as the images, running in parallel. No dialogue, no argument, just pattern recognition at a scale that rewires how you see cities.

Neri Oxman

Oxman's work sits exactly at the edge where biology becomes architecture becomes code — material computation as design language. Her projects collapse the distinction between grown and built, digital and physical, and carry the obsessive depth of someone who redesigned the entire problem before solving it.

Disintegration Loops
William Basinski

Made by accident — magnetic tape slowly destroying itself while recording — it's one of the most honest documents of entropy ever made, and became accidentally elegiac on September 11, 2001. The system eating itself as the medium, the content, and the meaning simultaneously.
