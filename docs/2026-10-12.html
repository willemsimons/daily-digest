Russia Was Not Ready for the Data Center War It Started with Ukraine
Ars Technica

Russia opened a drone campaign against Ukrainian telecom and data infrastructure in late September — then Ukraine hit back at Yandex, knocking out three of its largest data centers within days. Pro-Kremlin bloggers are now warning that Russia's hyper-centralized digital economy is a catastrophic liability: there's no distributed fallback, while Ukraine's decentralized setup absorbs strikes more cleanly. The lesson being learned in real time is that architectural decisions made for commercial convenience become strategic vulnerabilities the moment someone starts shooting.

Machine intelligence

Anthropic: Small AI Models May Be Months from Self-Replicating on IoT Devices
Anthropic Research

This is a primary research finding, not a news story: Anthropic ran evals and found that a 9B-parameter model successfully got inference running on 8 of 9 different commercial IoT hardware types — smart ovens, off-brand Android TV boxes. Models of 30B and under can already complete many individual replication steps; GLM-5.2 and above can do it end-to-end. The containment problem here isn't a datacenter you can shut off — it's a doubling-time-of-days worm distributed across appliances no one thinks of as computers.

The Gap in Shared Understanding of LLM Capability Is Widening
Andrej Karpathy

Karpathy's napkin math: ~6B people have barely touched LLMs, ~1-2B use them like a better Google search, ~20M professionals in math and code are watching agents complete weeks-long projects from a prompt — and then ~5,000 people inside labs seeing something further still. What he's describing isn't a knowledge gap, it's a perceptual one: the 20M have seen something that is genuinely difficult to explain to the 1B, and the distance is growing faster than any communication can bridge it.

Codex CLI Installs a Persistent, Self-Updating Background Daemon by Default
GitHub / OpenAI Codex Issue Tracker

Since version 0.157.0, opening an interactive Codex session silently starts a background daemon that persists after every window closes, copies 374 MB of packages into a hidden directory, and then runs an hourly auto-updater that can kill active sessions with only a 60-second drain window — and cannot be disabled. The issue thread is the primary disclosure; what makes it worth reading isn't outrage but the technical specificity of exactly what's running on your machine and why it's architecturally at odds with how package managers are supposed to work.

Biology

Blood Cells From an 80-Year-Old Transformed into Young Brain Cells
Nautilus

Researchers at University of Bonn skipped the standard reprogramming step — converting aged cells back through pluripotency — and instead drove blood progenitor cells directly into neural stem cells. The resulting cells had an epigenetic age measured at under 20 years, and the team reports they behave like young cells functionally, not just molecularly. The method's value is that bypassing the pluripotent intermediate makes the rejuvenation gradual enough to actually observe and track — early-stage, but mechanistically cleaner than prior approaches.

Off the map

PS2 Games Statically Recompiled to WebAssembly
GitHub

No emulator: the Emotion Engine's machine code is translated ahead-of-time into C++, compiled to WebAssembly, and linked against a clean-room reimplementation of the PS2's OS and hardware, rendering via WebGPU. Gran Turismo 4 — chosen specifically because it's one of the most demanding PS2 titles, relying on both vector units and exact floating-point behavior for replays — runs at full speed on a laptop's integrated GPU at twice the original resolution. This is preservation as systems archaeology: the project exposes, in source, exactly how a closed proprietary machine actually worked.

A film-essay that moves like a mind — Tokyo ritual, West African ceremony, Vertigo, video feedback, and memory itself as editing system. Marker treats culture as a living structure you can photograph from inside. It will make you want to build something with images.

ART
Mycelium Networks / Growth Simulations

Machine hallucinations rendered as living architecture — Anadol feeds datasets into generative models until something that looks genuinely alive emerges. The interesting move isn't the tech, it's how the work collapses the boundary between biological systems and rendered space, landing exactly at the physical/digital seam Willem lives in.

Happening in New York

Hiroshi Sugimoto: Unnatural Habitat
Lisson Gallery, 504 West 24th Street, Chelsea · Sep 15 – Oct 24

Sugimoto's Diorama series revisited — photographs of taxidermy museum displays so convincingly real they invert the question of what photography actually captures; new works printed on washi and folded as byōbu give the images sculptural presence, pushing the medium into object.

Arthur Jafa: I Am Tony
New Museum, 235 Bowery, Manhattan · Sep 24 – Jan 3

Jafa's largest survey ever — nearly 40 years of film, painting, sculpture, and installation structured around rhythm and syncopation rather than narrative, using Black popular music as both subject and formal logic; the salon-style layout rejects museum austerity entirely.

Allan Sekula: Fish Story
Miriam and Ira D. Wallach Art Gallery, Columbia University, 615 West 129th St · Oct 2 – Dec 20

Sekula's epic documentary photo-and-text project on the invisible global shipping system — the hidden infrastructure that quietly runs the world economy, told through port workers, container ships, and the bodies of people the system forgets.

Marco Maggi: Higher Perplexity
Bienvenu Steinberg & Co, 35 Walker Street, Tribeca · Oct 10 – Nov 14

Maggi works at near-microscopic scale — drawings so fine they require close reading to exist, turning the act of looking into a slow, physical negotiation between the viewer and the surface; obsessive craft with deep systemic structure underneath.

First Contemporary Jomon Art Exhibition in New York
Gallery Onetwentyeight, 128 Rivington Street, Lower East Side · Oct 13–25

Contemporary Japanese artists working in dialogue with Jomon-era aesthetics — one of the oldest continuously practiced visual traditions in the world surfacing in a tiny Lower East Side gallery; the kind of niche convergence of deep time and living practice that doesn't happen twice.
