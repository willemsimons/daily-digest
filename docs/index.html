The proof

The Riemann Hypothesis is proved — and machine-checked
Lean/Mathlib Community

Levent Alpöge, an Anthropic mathematician working with Claude, released a Lean 4 proof of the Riemann Hypothesis yesterday — 17 million lines of code, now compiled and verified correct by dozens of independent researchers within 24 hours. The community has certified all four criteria: correct statement, clean compilation, proof of the right theorem, and no dependency outside Mathlib. The deepest open problem in mathematics, standing since 1859, is closed — and the closure was machine-checked before most people woke up.

math216.wordpress.com

While Alpöge's proof was being verified, mathematician Alessandro Masullo — working entirely without AI, with months of feedback from Brian Conrad — also released a claimed proof of GRH on the same day, in an urgent pre-announcement from an AMS board meeting. Two simultaneous independent claims after 167 years of silence is not coincidence; something shifted in the mathematical environment this year.

Signal and noise

2 million canaries in the AI supply chain — and a lot of them sang
Ars Technica

Researcher HD Moore seeded 2 million unique, never-previously-used credential tokens into prompts sent through AI routing services like OpenRouter and Vercel — brokers that arbitrage inference across providers by cheapest price — and monitored his own infrastructure for any activity. The canaries sang: sensitive data leaked across the supply chain at scale, with credentials being actively exploited. If you use any AI tool that isn't a direct API call to a named model, your prompts may be traveling through infrastructure with no accountability.

METR's AI trajectory graph, updated — where the exponential holds and where it doesn't
MIT Technology Review

METR's updated time-horizon plot — the one showing frontier models doubling their completable task-length every four months — now includes longer-horizon and more open-ended tasks, and the finding is clarifying: exponential improvement holds for verifiable tasks (coding, math), but models underperform on tasks requiring judgment where success is hard to measure automatically. The gap between 'can it pass the test' and 'can it do the thing' is the most important unresolved question in applied AI right now.

Off the map

What peahens actually want — and what it means for how taste works
Nautilus

The standard story is that peacock tails are fitness signals — peahens choose the most symmetrical tail because it correlates with genetic health. A new chapter in evolutionary biology argues that's too reductive: peahens may be selecting for perceptual and aesthetic complexity itself, suggesting that taste in biological systems emerges from something closer to pleasure in sensory richness than rational fitness calculation. Darwin actually believed this — that animals experience beauty — and it was quietly suppressed by his successors. If true, aesthetic preference formation is not a shortcut for something else; it's the thing.

What do stem-like T cells have to do with why Type 1 diabetes never stops?
UF / Journal of Clinical Investigation

UF Diabetes Institute researchers publishing in the Journal of Clinical Investigation identified a population of stem-like T cells in human pancreas-draining lymph nodes that appear to continuously self-renew and replenish the autoimmune attack — which is why T1D doesn't resolve the way other immune responses do. 'That is what is perpetuating autoimmunity as a chronic condition,' said lead researcher Todd Brusko. It's preliminary in the sense that it opens drug target pathways rather than closing them, but it's the first mechanistic explanation for the chronicity that's been confirmed in human tissue.
