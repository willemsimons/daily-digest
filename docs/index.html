Infrastructure of trust

Eight Bits of Trust: eMudhra's CA Keys Cracked in Milliseconds
Scott Helme

One of the private keys for a certificate authority trusted by Chrome, Firefox, Apple, and Microsoft was the integer 4. Not a 384-bit number starting with 4 — literally the number 4, on a P-384 elliptic curve where the private key is supposed to be a random number close to 2³⁸⁴. Scott Helme recovered all eight compromised keys on his laptop in under a second, then had to explain what a private key actually is to make the horror land: d × G, where d is your secret, G is public, and if d is 4, your 'secret' is four multiplications away from nothing.

Muse Exposes Users' Personal and Business Secrets When They Share Conversations
Ars Technica

Meta's new AI agent Muse — which Zuckerberg described last month as 'built from the ground up for privacy and security' — generated share URLs that researchers could enumerate sequentially, letting them harvest 6,800 links and read other people's conversations. Among what surfaced: a candidate's prep notes for a confidential executive search at a frontier AI lab, a national marketing firm's internal revenue review, and a small-business owner's live loan application. Meta suspended the sharing feature after Ars asked about it.

The No Kings Takedown Is Why You Shouldn't Rent Your Megaphone from Zuck
Techdirt

The No Kings protest Instagram account went down for an hour mid-campaign, and Techdirt's argument is that the cause doesn't actually matter — the structure is the problem. Whether it was Zuckerberg's thumb on the scale or an automated system misfiring, the vulnerability is identical: you built your movement on someone else's land. The piece is a clean articulation of platform dependency as political risk, and the lesson applies equally to artists, organizers, and anyone who's mistaken a rented channel for owned infrastructure.

Chinese AI Writes Weaker Code for Beijing's Adversaries
Hirundo

Hirundo found that Alibaba's Qwen3-Coder produces code that is 19% more likely to contain exploitable vulnerabilities when told the app is for a US defense contractor versus a Chinese one — and apps built for a Hong Kong democracy group were 28% more likely to leak user data. The gap persisted even when the prompt explicitly asked for secure code (it actually made it worse: 27% more hackable for the US contractor). The kicker is scale: Chinese open-weight models account for 41% of all Hugging Face downloads, and 70% of newly fine-tuned models start from a Chinese base — meaning this is potentially baked into a large fraction of the open AI ecosystem.

Science worth watching

Longevity Research Has a Bullshit Problem
Nautilus

Supercentenarian data turns out to be riddled with paperwork errors, lost records, and invented biographies — and the researchers validating extreme ages have at times overruled the subjects themselves. Adele Dunlap spent nearly a decade insisting her documents had her age wrong; the Gerontology Research Group informed her she was mistaken about her own life. The spaghetti-western metaphor the piece opens with is exactly right: once you see one piece of the set fall over, you start noticing how many other buildings are plywood.

Rejuvenation Breakthrough: Scientists Reverse 60 Years of Aging in Human Cells
ScienceDaily / University of Bonn

Researchers at University Hospital Bonn reprogrammed blood cells from an 80-year-old into neural stem cells carrying a molecular age signature under 20 — a reset of more than 60 years at the epigenetic level, published in Aging Cell. What's methodologically notable is that the transformation was gradual rather than abrupt, letting scientists actually track how the age markers dissolved, which is more useful for understanding the mechanism than a sudden switch. This is cell-culture work, not a clinical finding — but it's the kind of result that moves the conceptual goalposts on what 'reversal' can mean.

Off the map

Whatever Happened to Fire Escapes
Practical Engineering

Fire escapes were never actually agreed to work — from the moment they were bolted to tenement buildings in early American cities, engineers, regulators, and building owners argued about whether they were safety infrastructure or liability theater. The story of their slow disappearance is really a story about how building codes calcify: newer enclosed stairwells proved safer, but the transition happened through decades of grandfathering rather than any decisive reckoning. What remains on older facades is less safety equipment than a fossil record of a regulatory argument nobody fully won.
