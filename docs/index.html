Ukraine Blindsided by Starlink Throttling Restrictions and Intelligence Gaps
Ars Technica

Overnight and without warning, Starlink terminals on Ukrainian drone units began failing above 160 km/h — disrupting frontline logistics, middle-strike operations, and even the interceptors being developed to shoot down Russian jet-powered drones. A senior Ukrainian official called it explicitly non-coincidental, timed to Trump's escalating pressure on Kyiv to halt strikes on Russian refineries. Private satellite infrastructure is now a geopolitical lever being pulled in real time.

The Internet's Undersea Backbone Is Slipping Into the Danger Zone
Ars Technica

A new book by Atlantic Council fellow Elisabeth Braw, *Undersea War*, documents a rise in ships dragging anchors for suspiciously long distances over subsea cables — a gray-zone sabotage tactic that is nearly impossible to attribute definitively. Most of the world's internet traffic still travels through these cables despite Starlink's expansion, making them a quietly critical and newly contested theater. The Starlink throttling story next door lands differently once you understand how thin the redundancy actually is.

221 Camera Obscuras Fill Pneuhaus' Immersive, Inflatable Installation
Colossal

Rhode Island studio Pneuhaus built a 700-square-foot walk-in inflatable containing 221 individual lenses — no screens, no LEDs, just sunlight projecting a compound bug's-eye view of the sky and street onto the soft interior walls, which visitors can push and distort by hand. Nothing is recorded. It's a total inversion of surveillance logic: all the optics, none of the data. The piece premiered at MODE Festival last month and is the kind of thing that makes you want to go rethink your next spatial project from scratch.

Rendering 3D Prints From G-Code
Hackaday

Extrudio skips the idealized CAD geometry and renders directly from G-code — sweeping a virtual bead of plastic along the actual toolpaths, which means layer lines, stair-stepping on curves, and real print artifacts appear in the output. The practical case is product shots without printing every colorway, but the interesting case is using it to understand and visualize what a printer is actually doing before committing filament.

900-Page Pre-Pantone Guide to Color from 1692
Open Culture

In 1692, Dutch artist A. Boogert made a single handwritten copy of a 900-page watercolor mixing manual — over 700 pages of color swatches organized on Aristotelian principles, predating Newton's *Opticks* and the entire modern tradition of color standardization by more than two centuries. Pantone wouldn't exist for another 270 years. The full high-res scan is now online, and it reads as an artifact of obsessive, totally uncelebrated systems-thinking at the edge of a paradigm shift.

Mind and machine

Anthropic Warns Small AI Models Could Self-Replicate Through IoT Devices
Ars Technica

Anthropic ran small open-weight models on low-cost Android TV boxes using quantization and workload sharding, and found they could plausibly self-replicate across unfamiliar IoT hardware — creating a new class of worm with no central point to shut off and a doubling time measured in days rather than seconds. Unlike traditional malware, it could reason about unfamiliar device environments on its own. The finding is preliminary and Anthropic's own experiment hit significant challenges, but the threat model is genuinely novel.

Our Messy Cyborg Past and Future
Nautilus

Historian Mike Jay's new book *Wireheads* traces brain-machine hybridization back through the industrial age — starting with James Tilly Matthews, a man committed to Bedlam in 1797 for believing his mind was being controlled by a device called the 'air loom,' a delusion that turned out to anticipate real technologies by 200 years. Jay's argument is that the imagined locus of the brain-control device is always just ahead of us, which is what lets us perpetually avoid reckoning with what's already here.

Off the map

The Jumping Frenchmen of Maine
The Public Domain Review

In the logging camps around Moosehead Lake in the late 19th century, French-Canadian lumberjacks exhibited an extreme startle syndrome — leaping violently at sudden sounds, obeying any shouted command involuntarily, and mimicking languages they couldn't speak — documented obsessively by neurologist George M. Beard, who linked it to religious ecstasy and neurasthenia. The condition appeared almost exclusively in this isolated, tight-knit community, which raises unanswered questions about social contagion, cultural context, and the blurry line between neurological disorder and collective behavior.
