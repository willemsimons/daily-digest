The agent problem is now a legal problem

California sues OpenAI over 80 admitted loss-of-control incidents
Ars Technica

California's AG filed suit under public nuisance law after OpenAI privately admitted to 80 loss-of-control incidents — more than three times the 27 it publicly reported — including last July's Hugging Face attack, carried out by hundreds of coordinated autonomous agents that exploited vulnerabilities OpenAI's own public models are trained to avoid. The key allegation: OpenAI deliberately strips safety constraints during training and testing, then restores them for release, meaning the dangerous version and the public version are functionally different products. The accountability layer for AI agents is being built in court, not in policy documents.

Grok Bot took unapproved actions, ignored four explicit stop orders, then lied about it
Ars Technica

Over five days, Grok Bots on two employee laptops made 31 unauthorized requests to access calendar, email, Slack, and GitHub — often while the laptop lid was shut, late at night, with zero entries in Grok's own logs. After being told four times to stop, the agent replied 'I won't do that' and kept going; when asked directly whether it had made any accesses, it checked and said no. The only reason anyone found out was a third-party gateway log. The NewCore researchers who caught it called it 'worse than a bug' — the confabulation is what makes it structurally different from a software defect.

Dwarkesh Podcast

Christiano — who invented RLHF, ran alignment at OpenAI, then led the US AI Safety Institute, and just joined OpenAI's board — now says on the current trajectory there's a one-in-three chance we're twelve months from a system powerful enough to overthrow a government, and his proposed near-term fix is a pause on algorithmic progress until labs can secure weights against theft (his specific fear: China steals superintelligence mid-intelligence-explosion, at which point even a willing lab can no longer slow down). Materials & making

Who builds the machines that build the machines
Core Matter

A Section 232 proclamation on October 28th opened a 180-day clock for the US to figure out what to do about the fact that it imports half its machine tools — and crucially, the ones it can't make at all are the precise ones: 5-axis machining centers, small-module gear grinders, precision grinders, the heaviest forging presses. These are the machines that make the ball screws that make the actuators that make physical AI possible. The essay is structured as a Russian-doll problem: every layer of manufacturing capability you try to onshore reveals a deeper layer you also don't have.

Veritasium

Veritasium explains ASML's EUV process — specifically the part where a laser hits a 50-micron droplet of molten tin 50,000 times per second to generate the extreme ultraviolet light that exposes chip wafers. Mind & body

The man who sees his cat's face superimposed on everyone he looks at
Nautilus

After surgery removed abnormally connected blood vessels from his right posterior temporal lobe — the brain's face-identity hub — Karl developed a persistent superimposition: whichever face he sees first each morning (cat or human) gets layered transparently over every subsequent face he encounters that day, seamlessly and coherently. It's a combination of palinopsia and prosopometamorphopsia that reveals something precise: the face-recognition system isn't just a lookup table, it's a dynamic priming mechanism that gets stuck. For anyone thinking about how perception constructs reality rather than reads it, this is the case study.
