The OpenAI Bug: Espionage at the Center of AI
Ars Technica

A Saudi engineer wrapped a USB recording device in toilet paper and plugged it into the back of a conference room TV inside OpenAI's most restricted executive space — a room where leadership discussed government partnerships and technical progress. OpenAI took two days to find it, then worked with the FBI quietly for days before pulling his access. The structural story isn't the crime; it's that concentrating frontier AI capability in one building creates a national-security espionage surface that didn't exist five years ago.

Solar Inverters Are an Unguarded Back Door into the European Grid
Bert Hubert's writings

Over 100 gigawatts of European rooftop solar are centrally managed from poorly secured, non-European clouds — and transmission operators say a 3GW disruption would take down their grid. Bert Hubert went to Brussels for a major EU cybersecurity forum specifically to raise this, and found the room full of every relevant regulator and operator in Europe, yet the subject was barely touched. The gap between known systemic risk and political will to act is the story.

Biology at the edge

Pig Kidneys Frozen for a Year, Then Transplanted Successfully
MIT Technology Review

Researchers vitrified pig kidneys — cooling them to –150°C into a glass-like state — stored one for over a year, rewarmed them, and transplanted them into pigs, where the kidneys regained color and produced urine. The lead surgeon said walking out after the last transplant: "This is a big deal." It's a preprint, not yet peer-reviewed, and long-term viability hasn't been proven — but if it holds, it breaks the transplant bottleneck entirely: organs could be banked like blood.

AI Designed Proteases That Cut Specific Proteins — Including Disease-Linked Ones
Ars Technica

Peptide bonds — the links that hold proteins together — have a half-life estimated at several hundred years without enzymatic help. Researchers took the catalytic core of two known protease types, stripped everything else, and used AI design tools to rebuild the surrounding structure from scratch, producing enzymes tunable to cut specific target proteins. They demonstrated this by cleaving proteins associated with neurological diseases — essentially programmable molecular scissors, designed de novo.

Making and unmaking software

AI Is Mass-Decompiling Games — Every Closed Classic Is Becoming Remixable
Simon Willison's Weblog

AI decompilation tools are quietly dissolving the line between shipped binary and source code — games are being ported, cross-bred, and preserved at a scale that wasn't possible a year ago. The same capability applies to any software: competitors can reverse-engineer products, paid apps can be open-sourced against their makers' will. The preservation upside is real — old games running in a browser via WebAssembly, unplayable-due-to-server-shutdown titles rescued — but the IP implications are industry-wide and nobody has a clean answer.

Off the map

In Patagonia, AM Radio Is the Social Network — And Has Been for Decades
The New Yorker

Twice a day across Argentina's remote steppe, major AM stations broadcast short personal messages to pastoralists with no internet or cell service — a gaucho fifty miles from the nearest village whacking his receiver until it stops crackling to hear if anyone has left word for him. "Mensajes al Poblador Rural" has been running for decades, and it functions exactly like a social network: presence signals, community binding, peer-to-peer reach mediated by a shared broadcast layer. The anti-algorithm social graph, built on physics and necessity.

For you

FILM
Sans Soleil
Chris Marker

A first-person essay-film that wanders between Tokyo, Guinea-Bissau, and Iceland, using the camera as a tool for interrogating how memory, ritual, and image-making shape identity — the closest cinema has come to thinking out loud about invisible cultural systems. If you haven't seen it, cancel your evening.

ART
Flatland (and the broader practice)
Vera Molnár

Molnár spent decades hand-coding generative drawings before personal computers existed, obsessively varying a single parameter at a time to understand how disorder emerges from rule — pure systems thinking rendered as sensory pleasure, and still the most honest statement about what it means to make generative art.

Daniel Lopatin assembled this album entirely from sampled TV commercials — advertising detritus reconstructed into something mournful and alien. Make something

Build a one-page, single-screen 'broadcast layer' — a webpage that displays a stream of short, hand-written messages (no likes, no replies, no accounts) in the style of Mensajes al Poblador Rural. Messages appear sequentially, timed, and disappear. The constraint: no interactivity except submitting a new message via a plain text field. Think about what changes when a social layer has no memory and no feedback loop — only presence and timing.
