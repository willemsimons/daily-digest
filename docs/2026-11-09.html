How perception actually works

Your Brain Flickers Between Two Realities 15 Times a Second
ZME Science / UC Berkeley & Princeton

Using Neuropixels probes during binocular rivalry — where each eye sees a different image — Janis Hesse and Doris Tsao found that neurons alternate roughly 15 times per second between representing what you consciously see and what your brain is actively suppressing from awareness. The implication is that consciousness isn't a place in the brain but a timing phenomenon: awareness is what happens when neural firing crosses a temporal threshold, not when it reaches a particular region.

A Visual Illusion Shows What AI Vision Still Can't Do
York University / Current Biology

The motion aftereffect — stare at a moving thing, then a stationary object appears to drift the opposite direction — turns out to reveal a history-dependent computation in primate IT cortex where objects are encoded in perceptually-shifted coordinates, not just retinal ones. Current AI vision networks simply don't do this: they process each frame statelessly, which is why they're simultaneously superhuman at recognition tasks and blind to exactly the kind of context-dependent, temporally-integrated perception that biological vision runs on.

Body and mechanism

A Mitochondrial Protein Nobody Was Watching May Drive Insulin Resistance
ScienceDaily / Pennington Biomedical Research Center

Miro1, a GTPase sitting on the outer mitochondrial membrane, accumulates in skeletal muscle in people with Type 2 diabetes — and in preclinical models, removing it restored insulin sensitivity. The kicker: exercise was found to lower Miro1 levels in humans with diabetes, which offers a molecular mechanism for why physical activity improves blood sugar control that's entirely separate from the GLP-1 pathway.

Off the map

Physicists Listened to Electrical Noise to Solve a 40-Year Superconductor Mystery
Ars Technica

In cuprate superconductors — the materials that superconduct at liquid-nitrogen temperatures and have baffled physicists since the late '80s — a strange state called the pseudogap causes electrons to apparently vanish. Milan Allan's team resolved a decades-long debate about why by measuring Johnson noise, the faint thermal crackle of current, and found that the missing electrons are forming Cooper pairs above the superconducting transition temperature, linking the pseudogap directly to superconductivity rather than treating them as separate phenomena. The elegance is that the 'garbage' signal — noise — was the instrument.

For you

FILM
Sans Soleil
Chris Marker

A film-essay that moves like a consciousness — Tokyo ritual, Guinea-Bissau revolution, Hitchcock, memory, and time, all held together by a system Marker invented from scratch. The form IS the argument. If you haven't seen it, it'll reorganize how you think about images and what they carry.

ART
Lucas Samaras: Boxes and Reconstructions
Lucas Samaras

Samaras spent decades building obsessive, jewel-encrusted boxes — objects that are simultaneously self-portraits, traps, and altars — and his 1979 Reconstructions fabric paintings, never widely seen, collapse high craft into something hallucinatory. The work sits exactly at the edge of object and body, system and mania.

MUSIC
Disintegration Loops
William Basinski

Magnetic tape decaying in real time, the signal eating itself — it's a system that runs to its own conclusion and takes beauty with it. Made accidentally during 9/11, the work is about entropy as composition. For someone who thinks in feedback loops and deep time, it hits differently than almost anything.
