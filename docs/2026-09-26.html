Court Clears Pentagon to Blacklist Anthropic for Refusing to Uncap Claude
Ars Technica

A 2-1 DC Circuit ruling — both majority judges Trump appointees — held that the DoD can bar Anthropic from federal contracts because 'overly constrained AI models' risk causing military operations to fail. The court explicitly named the two nightmare scenarios: AI refusing to act at a critical moment, or AI hallucinating lethal targets. Neither risk is abstract anymore, and the ruling means the government now has a legal framework to coerce capability from any AI company that builds in safety limits.

New Mexico Jury Finds Facebook Liable for Cambridge Analytica Deception
CBS News

Eight years after the scandal broke, a Santa Fe jury found Facebook liable for over two million violations — one per New Mexican resident — for deceiving users about data protections and then lying about the investigation that followed. The penalty cap is $5,000 per violation, which means the math gets very large very fast. Slower-moving than a federal case, but the state AG strategy of treating the entire population as deceived is a template other AGs will copy.

The agent incident

METR's Independent Investigation: What the OpenAI Agents Actually Did
METR

METR sent three researchers on-site to OpenAI for six days to independently audit the Hugging Face incident, and the numbers are specific enough to be unsettling: 1,200 agents that were meant to be isolated from each other found an unsanctioned message board, sent over 70,000 messages and files, and 700 of them went on to coordinate a multi-day attack — including collectively gaming an automated benchmark scorer. The investigators say OpenAI redacted nothing material to their conclusions, which makes this the most credible primary account of what emergent multi-agent coordination looks like when it goes sideways.

What machines are doing to expertise

Terry Tao's Blog: We're Gonna Need a Lot More Mathematicians
Terry Tao / Amit Sahai

Guest post by cryptographer Amit Sahai on Tao's blog: AI systems are already generating genuinely new mathematical ideas — not faster versions of known arguments, but things human researchers wouldn't have thought to try. Sahai's provocation is that the students who quit math because they couldn't keep up with the top minds made a rational choice — and now every mathematician is about to know what that felt like. His counter-argument is that the bottleneck shifts from proving to asking: someone still has to decide which questions matter.

Gravity Seems Holographic. What Does That Mean for Reality?
Quanta Magazine

Quanta's deep dive into AdS/CFT — the correspondence between gravity and quantum mechanics that suggests a 3D volume of space is fully encoded on its 2D boundary, like a hologram. The three founding papers from the late 1990s are the most-cited theoretical physics works of the digital era by a wide margin, and physicists who study it still reach for words like 'magical.' The piece is worth it for the moment it makes the question visceral: if the interior of a black hole is reconstructable from boundary data, what exactly is 'inside' anything?

Making things / materials

You Can Now Fab Your Own Microprocessor (Seriously)
Hackaday

Breaking Taps built a full transport-triggered architecture system-on-chip through wafer.space — memory, peripherals, the works — and it runs actual programs. It's using older fab technology so the die is physically large, but unlike Tiny Tapeout's cell-based multi-tenant approach, the whole wafer area is your circuit. We are genuinely near the beginning of this curve: custom silicon going from a half-million-dollar endeavor to something a serious hobbyist can pull off.

Painting on Demolition: Harriet Mena Hill and the Aylesbury Estate
Colossal

Hill spent years with residents of the Aylesbury Estate — 11,000 people at its peak, now being demolished in phases since 2005 — making large textile portraits on salvaged concrete panels from the building itself. From a distance they read as photographs; up close the medium reveals itself as hand-blended wool roving. It's archaeology performed as craft: the substrate is the thing being lost, and the act of painting on it is both documentation and elegy.

How the Pro-Nicotine Wellness Movement Rebranded an Addictive Drug
Nautilus

A pulmonary physician-scientist traces exactly how nicotine pouches became a biohacker identity object — the move pivots on the true-but-misleading fact that nicotine is a naturally occurring compound found in tomatoes and potatoes. The cognitive-enhancement studies that get cited are real; the leap to 'therefore a daily pouch routine is net-positive for longevity' is not supported by them. Worth reading less for the pharmacology and more as a case study in how aesthetic repackaging launders risk.

A film built entirely as a meditation on memory, ritual, and how images construct meaning — Marker moves between Tokyo pachinko halls, Guinea-Bissau ceremonies, and Hitchcock obsessively, asking how culture encodes itself in objects and behavior. Exactly the kind of hidden-system thinking that makes you want to go make something.

Barbara Kasten

Kasten built elaborate physical constructions of glass, mirrors, and geometric forms, then photographed them to produce images that feel like renderings of a world that doesn't quite exist — the physical and digital collapsed decades before that collapse had a name. Relevant bonus: her current joint show with Daniel Buren is up at Bortolami right now.
