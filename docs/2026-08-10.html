Institutions hollowing out

Peer Review Is Buckling — and AI Is Only Part of the Problem
Ars Technica

A health economist's paper on HPV vaccine mandates was rejected by a single reviewer who confused vaccine efficacy with policy effectiveness — two entirely different questions. Peer review's structural problem isn't dishonesty but collapse-by-volume: submissions have outrun the supply of willing, careful reviewers, so journals are accepting one-reviewer verdicts and AI-assisted reviews that pass superficially but miss the point. The knowledge-verification layer of science is quietly failing, and almost nobody outside the system is tracking it.

What Happened to HackerOne
teknogeek.io

In 2011, two hackers cold-emailed 100 tech giants, found bugs in Google, Facebook, and Apple, and built HackerOne to make ethical security research less legally terrifying. A decade later, the person who managed large bug bounty programs on both sides of that relationship describes a textbook arc: community trust traded for enterprise contracts, incentive structures quietly inverted, institutional memory gone. It's a story about what happens when a platform built on hacker values starts optimizing for a different customer.

AI for Science Needs Reasoning, Not Just Data
MIT Technology Review

AlphaFold solved a fifty-year-old protein structure problem by pattern-matching against known shapes — impressive, but it doesn't generalize to science that lacks large training sets or requires genuine hypothesis generation. The argument here is that what's missing isn't more data or bigger models but reasoning: the ability to model the actual epistemic process of research, including knowing when you don't know. Pairs uncomfortably well with the peer review collapse story above.

Geometry, deep time, and the shape of things

Mount Toba Didn't Almost Kill Us — New Lake Sediment Data Rewrites the Story
Ars Technica

74,000 years ago, Toba erupted with roughly 1,000 times the force of Pinatubo — and textbooks have said ever since that it nearly wiped out humanity. New sediment cores from a crater lake on the Kenya-Tanzania border show the cooling lasted under two years and amounted to maybe half a degree. The twist is physical: above a certain eruption size, aerosol droplets become too heavy and settle out of the stratosphere too quickly to sustain a volcanic winter, which means the biggest eruptions are paradoxically less catastrophic than mid-sized ones.

Even on Mars, Hexagons Are the Bestagons
Hackaday

NASA's Curiosity rover is rolling through a vast field of hexagonal polygonal fractures in Valle Grande — and the honest answer is nobody knows exactly how they formed. Dried mud, freeze-thaw cycling, and ancient hydrology are all on the table, which makes this both an open scientific question and a clue about Mars's geologically recent past. The field stretches as far as the camera sees: the same tiling pattern that falls out of soap bubbles, basalt columns, and Saturn's pole, now confirmed on another planet's surface.

Europe's Oldest Buddhist Temple, Built to Honor Napoleon's Enemies
Aeon Video

The Khosheutovsky Khurul sits on the Russian steppe as an architectural object that shouldn't exist — a Buddhist temple built in 1817 to commemorate a Kalmyk cavalry regiment that fought alongside Russian forces against Napoleon. Aeon's video essays consistently find the place where military history, diaspora identity, and sacred architecture collide; this one is built around an object that is simultaneously a war memorial, a religious site, and a relic of a nomadic people's survival across empires.

Off the map

Someone Is Bulk-Buying 5,000 Obscure Books from European Independents — Nobody Knows Why
Irish Times

Orders of 5,000 titles are arriving overnight at independent booksellers from Galway to Berlin — no haggling, €10,000–€50,000 a pop, mixing A History of Connemara with a 2018 driving test guide. The booksellers are cashing in but quietly terrified: a similar pattern swept US antiquarian dealers earlier this year, and the working theory is that someone is systematically sweeping physical copies of long-tail titles off the market, possibly to corner bibliographic scarcity. The motive — archival, speculative, adversarial — remains genuinely unknown.
