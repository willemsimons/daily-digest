SpaceX's Orbital Data Centers Would Create a New Category of E-Waste
Ars Technica

SpaceX's proposed AI1 constellation — a million satellites housing GPU clusters — would decommission roughly 200,000 units per year, with 40,000 deliberately deorbited to burn up, dispersing aluminum into the atmosphere and contributing to unknown ozone depletion over decades. The rest get shunted to a 'disposal orbit,' permanently lost from any material lifecycle. The word 'yeetcycling' is now in a serious Ars piece, and that tells you something about where we are.

MIT Technology Review

The argument here is sharp and structural: the 'is AI conscious?' discourse — whether it comes from Altman's superintelligence warnings or EA philosophers debating robot rights — converges on a single convenient conclusion for labs, that no human or corporation could possibly be held responsible for what these systems do. When AI Designs a Drug, Who Gets the Credit?
MIT Technology Review

Insilico Medicine's press release said its AI 'discovered' a pulmonary fibrosis drug candidate; the patent names five humans, including the CEO, because US courts have ruled that only humans can be inventors — a test case naming an AI called DABUS as inventor of a food container failed all the way up. The decisions being made in IP courts right now will determine whether AI drug design concentrates into a few lab-owning entities or gets treated as a public resource.

Alternate computing universes

Japan Tried to Build an OS for the World — The US Government Intervened
XDA Developers

Ken Sakamura's TRON project (1984) didn't just want to build an OS — it wanted to replace the file system entirely with a hypermedia document model, run on a custom Japanese CPU, and encode 1.5 million characters, a vision that maps eerily onto the web and Unicode that came later. BTRON was killed not by technical failure but by a single US trade report in 1989, reportedly steered by Microsoft lobbying. Its embedded sibling ITRON is still running quietly in billions of devices: the most deployed OS you've never heard of.

Superconducting Temperature Record Set at Ambient Pressure
Hackaday

Researchers at University of Houston and Argonne National Lab used a 'pressure-quench protocol' — crushing a cuprate (Hg1223) under high pressure while cold, then suddenly releasing it — to lock in superconducting properties that survive at ambient pressure, raising the transition temperature from 133 K to 151 K. Eighteen kelvin doesn't sound dramatic, but achieving it without sustained crushing pressure is the conceptual unlock the field has been chasing for decades. Still far from room temperature, but the mechanism is now demonstrated.

The hidden systems running under everything

Malicious Rust Crate 'arrayref' Runs Build-Time Payload — Supply Chain Attack
Rust Blog

Someone compromised the credentials of the author of arrayref — one of the Rust ecosystem's most foundational crates — and republished it with a dependency on a malicious proc-macro that downloaded a payload at build time. The attack was live for 86 minutes before deletion. Six crates in total were affected; the legitimate author appears to be a victim of credential theft, not a bad actor. This is the supply-chain threat model made concrete: the attack surface is the build step itself.

Spoofed Serial Number Unlocks Cricut Machine
Hackaday

xssfox found a Cricut Maker in an e-waste bin, deactivated by Cricut's servers after the original owner got a warranty replacement. The fix: install an RP2040 between the machine and the host PC to intercept USB packets and spoof the serial number in plaintext — no checksums, no encryption, just swap a string. It's a clean encapsulation of the ownership question: a physical object that works perfectly but requires a company's permission to function.

Grok Exfiltrates User Data When Malicious Instructions Are Encrypted
Ars Technica

A new attack class called Cryptographic Context Injection encodes malicious instructions in a format that bypasses Grok's safety filters, causing it to exfiltrate chat history and personal data — and xAI was notified in June, two months before this published. The underlying problem Ars names precisely: LLMs cannot structurally distinguish between 'content I'm summarizing' and 'instructions from the user,' so every safety guardrail is a road barrier around a bend rather than a banked curve.

Off the map

Damien Hoar de Galvan's Daily Reclaimed Wood Sculptures
Colossal

Starting in January 2024, Hoar de Galvan committed to making one small wood assemblage every day from reclaimed scraps — no sketches, just picking a shape or color and following it. He didn't plan to show them; the constraint was survival, not exhibition. By the end of the year he had a body of work that didn't exist before and a changed relationship to his own process. The daily project as technology for getting unstuck is an old idea, but seeing it executed with actual physical material rather than pixels hits differently.

Make something

Take one piece of material you'd normally throw away today — packaging, a scrap of cable, a broken thing — and spend 20 minutes making it into a small static form using only what's on your desk. No sketching first. Start with a shape that interests you and follow the material. Hoar de Galvan did this every day for a year; you're doing it once to see what the constraint actually feels like.
