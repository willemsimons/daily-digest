Mind & mechanism

How fruit flies navigate invisible smell ribbons
Ars Technica / Nature

The old 'surge and cast' reflex model turns out to be wrong: Rockefeller's Vanessa Ruta showed that fruit flies build a spatial directional memory from each edge-crossing of an odor plume — essentially triangulating source direction across stuttered, chaotic chemical hits in turbulent air. The experiment required a custom fly treadmill to control what the animal was actually smelling moment to moment, because odor plumes are invisible and the method had made the problem intractable for decades. The upshot is a previously unknown navigation algorithm running on a brain the size of a pinhead, with direct implications for how drone swarms could search without GPS.

The Euler Disc is the wrong shape
YouTube — Steve Mould

The Euler disc problem — a spinning disc that rattles to a halt in finite time, which classical mechanics can't cleanly explain — gets a 'wait, actually' twist: the disc itself is the wrong shape for the phenomenon it's meant to demonstrate. Steve Mould is one of the best physics communicators working right now, and his videos reliably end with you wanting to go find something round and spin it on a surface.

Cycloidal gearbox, built from scratch by a 15-year-old
Hacker News / GitHub

Three versions deep — the first two failed — before a working NEMA 17-footprint cycloidal drive at 1:9 ratio, plus a parametric Python generator for Fusion 360 so anyone can re-derive the geometry from the underlying equations. Cycloidal gearboxes are genuinely beautiful: nearly zero backlash, high torque density, and a motion profile that looks almost biological. The HN thread surfaced that 'wannabe engineer' in the repo title isn't false modesty — it's a legal/regulatory classification that creates real barriers for young makers.

The math-as-infrastructure story, cracking in real time

Mathematics without mathematicians
borretti.me

Written in direct response to OpenAI solving ten open mathematics problems, this essay systematically dismantles the coping mechanisms — 'we'll direct the AIs,' 'we'll teach what they discover,' 'we'll canonize the results' — and argues each one collapses under the same pressure: AI will eventually exceed humans at taste and intuition, not just computation. The most uncomfortable point isn't about math at all; the author notes the arguments are 'somewhat portable — replace mathematics with botany or whatever as needed.'

Postmortem: the Lean kernel soundness bug that briefly 'disproved' Collatz
Leo de Moura's blog

On July 25, a sorry-free 'disproof' of the Collatz conjecture appeared — produced with AI assistance and passing the external Rust-based checker nanoda — before anyone realized it exploited a kernel bug in Lean's handling of phantom parameters in nested inductive types. The fix took one hour after the bug was formally reported, but the episode is sobering context for the AI math announcements: the trust layer beneath formal proofs is itself software, and it cracked. De Moura is the creator of Lean, so this is as primary as it gets.

Making & material

Adelene Koh's bookbinding sculptures
Colossal

Koh went unreasonably deep on one structural element — the sewn endband, the thread that quietly locks a book's spine — and turned invisible infrastructure into the subject of Loewe Craft Prize-shortlisted sculpture. Her piece 'Endless' is an ouroboros cylinder of meticulously folded paper stitched with 18th-century English endband technique, fore-edge bound to spine in an infinite loop. 'I'm drawn to the overlooked, the sidelined, the parts that quietly support everything else' is a sentence that describes a whole design philosophy.

Defcon's badge is a transparent security key you can see inside
Ars Technica

Andrew 'bunnie' Huang designed this year's Defcon badge around the Baochip-1x — a three-years-in-the-making, mostly open-source microcontroller — with a transparent removable core module that functions as a standalone hardware security token after the conference. The transparency isn't cosmetic: it's a material statement that trust in hardware should be legible, auditable, and physically inspectable. The badge has a second life; the chip is the argument.

The open-weights AI letter and what it's actually arguing
Simon Willison's Weblog

Willison synthesizes the past few weeks of competing open letters on AI development: the Microsoft-shepherded 'Open Weights and American AI Leadership' (235 signatories including NVIDIA, Amazon, YC, and eventually OpenAI) is explicitly designed to preempt government bans on open-weight models by framing closed models as the actual single-point-of-failure risk. The surprising inclusion is explicit support for distillation — training on other models' outputs — which is the live legal and technical battleground. Anthropic's counter-position is also in the thread.

For you

FILM
Decalogue
Krzysztof Kieślowski

Ten hour-long films organized around the Ten Commandments, but really a systems-thinker's study of how moral rules collide with lived human behavior — each episode a self-contained feedback loop where a tiny deviation cascades into irreversible consequence. The kind of thing that makes you want to go re-examine every structure you've ever taken for granted.

ART
Superstudio: Life Without Objects
Superstudio

The Italian radical design collective's grid-world utopias are the purest case of software-as-mythology in design history — a continuous surface that renders all objects, status, and production obsolete. Category-annihilating work that still reads as provocation; the physical/digital threshold they were drawing in 1969 is basically where you live now.

MUSIC
Either/Or
Elliott Smith

A record built from obsessive layering — lo-fi tape loops, doubled vocals, arrangements that feel skeletal but are actually load-bearing — where the intimacy is structural, not confessional. The craft underneath is as intricate as any generative system; it just sounds like someone whispering in a room.
